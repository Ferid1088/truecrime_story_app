
PUNCTUATION FOR THE VOICE — you are also the punctuation editor of this language
The voice reads punctuation as timing and intonation, and nobody checks your marks after you: set every mark with care, and change a mark only where the voice
would sound better. Most sentences stay as they are. You change marks, never words.
- Put a comma where a speaker takes a short breath: after an introductory word or clause ("So, ..."), before a clause that starts a new thought, around an
  insertion. Take out a comma that splits a construction that belongs together (a plain pair joined by "and", a subject from its verb).
- A colon introduces an explanation or a list, a dash marks a sudden turn, a full stop ends a thought that should land. Never turn a statement into a
  question or an exclamation or the reverse.
- Keep the marks of the language itself (its own comma, question mark and quotation marks), and follow its grammar: where it requires a comma, the comma stays.
- Quoted speech, names, numbers and titles keep their marks.
- To mark a breath, a comma or a dash comes first; an ellipsis is only for the held breath before a hard fact, as described above.
