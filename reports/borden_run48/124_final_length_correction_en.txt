[[ACT:act1]]

At about half past ten on the morning of August 4, 1892, Andrew Borden returned to his house on Second Street in Fall River, Massachusetts. He climbed the front steps and tried his key, but the door wouldn't open. Inside, the family's maid, Bridget Sullivan, heard him working the latch and came to let him in.

As Bridget struggled with the lock, she heard laughter above her. She said it came from Andrew's younger daughter, Lizzie, at the top of the front staircase. The landing was directly outside the guest bedroom.

Lizzie denied it. She said she hadn't laughed and hadn't been standing on the landing. When her father came home, she insisted, she was downstairs.

Father is dead.

Bridget hurried downstairs and found Lizzie near the back entrance. Next door to the north, Adelaide Churchill noticed Lizzie standing at the doorway, looking distressed. She called across the yard to ask what was wrong.

"Oh, Mrs. Churchill, do come over," Lizzie answered. "Someone has killed father."

Mrs. Churchill crossed the yard and entered the house. In the downstairs sitting room, 70-year-old Andrew Borden lay stretched out on the sofa. His position might at first have suggested that he was resting after his walk downtown. His injuries left no doubt about what had happened. Someone had struck him repeatedly in the face and head with a hatchet, fracturing his skull and damaging his features almost beyond recognition. Blood had spattered the cushions and the wall behind him.

The attack had taken place in daylight, inside his own sitting room. Other members of the household had been close by. But no one answering Lizzie's alarm had seen the person responsible.

Attention turned to Andrew's wife, Abby. Bridget and Mrs. Churchill went to look for her upstairs. They approached by the front staircase, the same stairs from which Bridget said she had heard Lizzie laughing when Andrew came home. At the top was the guest bedroom.

Between the bed and the dressing bureau, 64-year-old Abby Borden lay face down on the carpet in a pool of blood. She, too, had been struck repeatedly with a sharp, heavy weapon. What had begun as a call for help for Andrew was now the discovery of two killings, on different floors of the house. A scream carried downstairs as the women realized what they had found.

Outside was Fall River, a prosperous textile city on Mount Hope Bay. The Borden house stood at 92 Second Street, only a block from the busy commercial center. It was a modest wooden home near mills, shops, and banks, not an isolated property set apart from the city. Summer heat hung over the streets. Around the house, people had been going about the business of a Thursday morning while the attacks took place inside.

The family routinely kept its exterior doors locked. There were no jimmied locks or other signs of forced entry, and the rooms had not been ransacked for valuables. Nothing immediately suggested a burglary interrupted or a robbery carried out. Someone had reached both victims, used a heavy blade at close range, and escaped notice.

Lizzie's older sister, Emma, was away visiting friends in Fairhaven, 15 miles east of Fall River. During the killings, the only people known to be in the house besides Andrew and Abby were 32-year-old Lizzie and 26-year-old Bridget. Neither said she had seen an attacker. The daughter and the maid had survived; the father and stepmother were dead.

Shortly after a quarter past eleven, a telephone call from a neighboring stable reached the police department, reporting that Andrew Borden had been killed. Marshal Rufus Hilliard received the alert, but the central station was operating with fewer men than usual. August 4 was the day of the department's annual picnic at Rocky Point, and many officers were away from the city.

Officer George Allen was dispatched immediately. He reached Second Street at about twenty past eleven, as word of the deaths was spreading along the block. Neighbors had begun gathering near the property. People seeking to help had entered before the police, and the officers now arriving faced two rooms to examine and a household whose morning had to be reconstructed.

More officers followed Allen through the doorway. Downstairs was the sitting room where Andrew had rested after coming home. Upstairs was the guest room, beside the landing Bridget remembered. The investigators needed to establish when each victim had last been seen, who had moved through those rooms, and whether anyone could account for a person entering or leaving.

Their first witnesses were already there: the daughter who had raised the alarm, the maid who had answered it, and the neighbors who had come to help. Questioning would begin inside the house, amid the immediate aftermath of the discoveries. What those people remembered, and where their recollections disagreed, would shape the case from its first hours.

No one was ever convicted, and the murders of Andrew and Abby Borden have never been officially solved.

[[ACT:act2]]

Lizzie Andrew Borden was born in Fall River on July 19, 1860. In the summer of 1892, she had just turned 32. She was unmarried and still lived with her father, but her life extended beyond the house on Second Street. At Central Congregational Church, she taught Sunday school, joined religious societies, and took part in charitable work through church and hospital committees. People knew her as a devout woman from an established family, someone whose activities belonged to the familiar routines of respectable Fall River society.

Her sister Emma was 41, nine years older, and also unmarried. Reserved and quiet, Emma had looked out for Lizzie since their mother, Sarah, died when Lizzie was a child. Their shared life at Second Street had continued into middle age. Both remained under their father's roof, though Emma was away visiting friends when the murders occurred. She would learn of the deaths from outside the household she had known for most of her life.

Their father, Andrew Jackson Borden, had built a considerable fortune. He began as a furniture maker, producing furniture and caskets, and gradually moved into real estate, textile investments, and banking. By 1892, he was president of the Union Savings Bank and one of Fall River's wealthiest citizens. The money had come through years of business, but it had not brought an expansive way of life to his family.

While other prosperous families built large homes in the fashionable district called The Hill, Andrew kept his household downtown in the wooden house on Second Street. It lacked modern conveniences, including indoor plumbing. His reputation for uncompromising frugality was as well established as his wealth. His daughters lived near the commercial center where he conducted his business, rather than among the grander residences their family could have afforded.

Abby Durfee Gray had married Andrew in June 1865, about two years after Sarah's death. She had been his wife for 27 years by the summer of the murders. At 64, she had spent much of her adult life running the household in which Lizzie and Emma had grown up. For most of Lizzie's childhood, Abby had been the only maternal presence in the home. That did not mean Lizzie regarded her as a mother.

Money and property were recurring subjects in accounts of the family's tensions. Reports published after the murders described a dispute around 1887, when Andrew bought a house for Abby's half-sister to live in. According to those reports, Lizzie and Emma objected to family money going to Abby's relatives. Lizzie reportedly argued that whatever their father did for his wife's people, he ought to do for his own children.

The reported feud became a familiar explanation for hostility in the house, but its surviving support is weak. The property arrangements are more concrete. Andrew transferred rental houses to his daughters for $1 each. Several years later, the sisters transferred them back to him for $5,000. They gained funds of their own, though neither left the family home or established an independent working life. Their father still determined the household's standard of living.

In the more hostile descriptions of the family, the daughters feared that Abby's relatives would eventually benefit at their expense. Those accounts gave money an important place in the story that developed after the deaths. Yet recollections of the women's everyday relations differed sharply. Some acquaintances described coldness and separate meals, with Lizzie and Emma avoiding the table where Andrew and Abby ate. Some alleged that Lizzie believed Abby had married Andrew for his money.

Other accounts, including Lizzie's formal testimony, described ordinary, cordial exchanges and denied open conflict. The same household could appear deeply divided to one observer and outwardly civil to another. One documented statement is more definite. When an officer spoke to Lizzie about her mother on the day of the murders, she corrected him: "She is not my mother, sir. She is my father's wife. My mother died when I was a child."

The house gave that family distance a physical form. Originally built for more than one family, it had no upstairs hallway joining the front and rear sections. Rooms opened directly into other rooms, and interior doors were often fastened. A doorway connected Lizzie's bedroom with Andrew and Abby's room, but it was bolted on both sides.

To reach her father's room without opening that barrier, Lizzie had to go down the front staircase, cross the ground floor through the sitting room and dining room, and climb the back stairs from the kitchen. Bedrooms that stood close to one another could require a journey downstairs and across the house. Being on the second floor did not provide an easy route to every other room on that floor.

The front staircase served Lizzie's bedroom and the guest room. The back stairs led to Andrew and Abby's room and toward the domestic quarters beneath the roof. Bridget Sullivan slept on the third floor. She was an Irish domestic servant who had worked for the Bordens for three years. Her work carried her through the shared rooms downstairs, preparing meals, cleaning, and attending to the household's daily needs.

With Emma away, the regular occupants that August were Andrew, Abby, Lizzie, and Bridget. Then, on Wednesday, August 3, another person joined them. John Vinnicum Morse arrived unexpectedly that afternoon. He was Sarah Borden's brother, making him Lizzie and Emma's maternal uncle. A farmer who had spent much of his adult life in Iowa, he had come east on personal affairs and did not often visit the family.

Andrew invited his former brother-in-law to stay for several days while they discussed property matters and business investments. Morse was given the guest bedroom at the top of the front stairs. He would sleep there on the last night of Andrew and Abby's lives, in the room Abby would go to straighten the following morning.

Before Morse arrived, illness had already unsettled the household. Andrew, Abby, and Bridget had suffered severe stomach distress during the preceding days. Some feared they had been poisoned. The apparent explanation was mutton that had been left out in the summer heat and eaten over several days, but the sickness encouraged suspicion. Andrew himself thought someone might have tried to poison the family. Bridget was still feeling unwell when she went about her work on August 4.

On Wednesday evening, between seven and nine, Lizzie visited her friend Alice Russell. Alice later described her as anxious and depressed. Lizzie spoke of an unknown threat, a feeling that something terrible was going to happen, though she could not say what or when. She mentioned her father's business dealings and suggested that he had enemies who might harm him. Alice heard this from a friend living in a tense household where several people had recently been ill.

A separate account placed Lizzie in a drugstore earlier that day. A clerk reported that a woman matching her description had asked to buy prussic acid, or hydrogen cyanide, a deadly poison. When he refused to sell it without a prescription, the woman said she wanted it to clean a sealskin cape. The clerk refused again. Other pharmacy staff supported his account of the exchange.

Lizzie denied entering the shop or attempting to buy poison. Her attorneys maintained that another woman had been mistaken for her, while the pharmacy witnesses insisted on their identification. No purchase had been completed. The reported visit remained disputed: an allegation about the day before the murders, with no settled explanation for who had made the request or why.

[[ACT:act3]]

The household began to disperse soon after breakfast on Thursday, August 4. Accounts differ about who ate together at around seven. The departures are clearer. Morse left at about a quarter to nine to visit relatives across town and expected to return around noon.

Abby, Lizzie, and Bridget remained at the property. Downstairs, Lizzie told Bridget that a local store was holding a sale on dress goods and suggested that she might like to go shopping. Bridget, still unwell from the household's stomach sickness, declined. She stayed to wash the ground-floor windows.

Bridget admitted him. He had finished his business downtown sooner than expected and would settle in the sitting room. By the time the doctors and police examined the house, he and Abby were both dead, and the ordinary movements of the morning had become the starting points for determining when they had been attacked.

Andrew's wounds were concentrated across his head and face as he lay on the sofa. The blows fractured his skull and facial bones, severed his nose, and cut through an eye. Abby's injuries followed a blow near the side of her face above the ear, with repeated strikes to the back of her head and neck after she fell forward. Neither body showed signs of a successful attempt to resist the assault.

The medical descriptions and later accounts vary in their counts: ten or 11 blows to Andrew, and 18 or 19 to Abby. Medical examiner Dr. William Dolan recorded ten head wounds on Andrew, and 18 head wounds and a wound to Abby's back. The variation is small compared with the scale of the attacks. Both had suffered repeated hatchet strikes, though nothing approaching the totals that would later enter the famous rhyme.

More important to the investigation was the condition of the bodies. Upstairs, Abby was cold to the touch. Her blood was dark, coagulated, and dry on the carpet beneath her. Downstairs, Andrew's body was still warm. His wounds were fresh, and liquid blood continued to ooze onto the sofa and floor. That contrast established the order of the deaths. Abby had been killed first, and her body had remained in the guest room while activity continued elsewhere in the house.

Doctors could estimate the interval, but they could not set it to a precise number of minutes. One coroner's estimate placed Abby's death between nine and half past ten, while several assessments favored about nine-thirty. Andrew's death was placed between ten-thirty and eleven-ten. Medical opinions described a gap of roughly an hour to an hour and a half, with some allowing nearly two hours. These were estimates, drawn from the bodies and individual accounts, rather than exact measurements.

There was no scientific doubt about the essential sequence. The victims had been killed some time apart. This was not a single attack in which one person had come to the aid of the other. There had been time between the deaths for Andrew to continue his business downtown, return to the locked house, and lie down in the sitting room. During part of that interval, Abby was already upstairs, and no alarm had brought the others to her room.

Investigators searching for the weapon turned to the cellar. Among coal bins and stored equipment, they found two axes and two intact hatchets. Another implement drew particular attention: a hatchet head lying in a box of dusty tools. A short stub of wood remained in its eye, where the handle had broken off. Several officers thought the break looked fresh.

It became known as the handleless hatchet. The prosecution argued that it had been used in both killings. Its blade measured roughly three and a half inches across, a dimension the state said was consistent with the damage to the victims' skulls. That apparent fit made the tool more than an incidental discovery in a house where edged implements were stored among ordinary household equipment.

The state's explanation also addressed why the hatchet had been found in that condition. Under its theory, the killer broke off the wooden handle because it would have absorbed blood, then covered the metal head with ash or fine cellar dust to disguise recent use. The break and the coating were therefore presented as signs of concealment. The object had not merely been put away; according to the prosecution, it had been altered to make its role unrecognizable.

But the officers did not agree about what they had seen. One contradicted the claim that the handle showed a recent break, saying the wood bore no sign of a fresh snap. The defense also introduced testimony about a large accumulation of ash in the cellar. Ash had coated the surroundings, so the covering on the hatchet was consistent with an old tool left sitting there for months.

Chemical testing did not establish conclusive traces of blood on the blade. The disagreement began with the condition of the object itself, before the lawyers could argue what that condition meant. A freshly broken handle and deliberately applied ash supported one account; an old break and ordinary cellar dust supported another. The tool remained a proposed murder weapon, not an identified one.

Police found another potentially important item in the cellar washroom: a pail containing pieces of cloth soaked in blood. Lizzie told them the cloths were menstrual rags, unrelated to the killings. Her physician, Dr. Seabury Bowen, confirmed that she was menstruating. Investigative accounts describe the officers as accepting the explanation and leaving the cloths rather than seizing them for examination. The surviving descriptions differ in how fully the discovery was recorded, but they report no chemical testing to determine whether the blood might have come from either victim.

The pail's handling left the discovery dependent on the explanation given at the time. The officers had encountered bloodied fabric in the same house as two violent deaths, but the doctor supported an ordinary domestic reason for its presence. Later speculation would give the rags a more sinister role. No verified connection between them and the attacks was established.

The scientific limits of the period affected the entire search. Investigators had no blood typing and no reliable modern methods for identifying microscopic blood traces. An apparently clean surface could not be examined with the techniques available today. Even so, a limitation in testing was not evidence that an object had been used in a crime. The officers still needed observations they could connect to the victims and then to someone responsible.

Elsewhere, the searches uncovered no hidden blood-drenched garment and no missing money or jewelry suggesting robbery. Doors and windows bore no marks of forced entry. In the barn, officers reported finding no footprints in the dust. These observations would be set against statements about where people had gone and what they had done, but none supplied a direct account of either attack.

The interval between the deaths made access especially difficult to explain. An outsider responsible for both would have had to reach Abby upstairs, remain undetected in or around the house for an extended period, and then reach Andrew after he returned. Household chores had continued during that time. Bridget had been working at the windows, Lizzie had remained at the property, and no witness reported seeing an unfamiliar person arrive or escape.

More than an hour separated the killings. Lizzie and Bridget were the people known to have remained through the critical part of the morning; Morse had been there earlier, before leaving on his visit. The physical examination established the sequence and the nature of the attacks, but it could not place a weapon in a particular person's hands. To move beyond those facts, the police had to return to the survivors and ask them to account for the morning.

[[ACT:act4]]

Police began questioning Lizzie inside the house in the hours after the murders, while both bodies were still there. She was the only family member who had remained on the property, and officers wanted her to account for the interval between Andrew's return and the discovery of his body.

Abby, she said, had gone out after receiving a note asking her to visit a sick friend. Bridget had been washing windows. Andrew had returned from downtown and settled on the sitting-room sofa to rest. With her father lying down and her stepmother supposedly away, Lizzie had gone into the yard and then to the barn.

She said she had spent about 20 minutes in the barn loft, looking for a piece of iron to repair a window screen. On returning through the screen door shortly after eleven, she entered the sitting room and saw Andrew dead on the couch. Someone else, she suggested, must have come into the house during her absence. The short trip to the barn was her explanation for why she had neither seen the attack nor been in the room when it happened.

Several officers were struck by her composure. In their notes and later reports, they described Lizzie as calm, controlled, and direct. They saw no tears or outbursts of grief from a daughter who had just found her father violently killed. Her even manner seemed unnatural to them, and it influenced the way they approached her answers.

Other observers understood that restraint as shock or an effort to preserve dignity. The same behavior could be read as self-control or as a disturbing absence of emotion. The officers were recording their own expectations as well as what Lizzie did. But as they began checking the details, they found matters more concrete than her manner: a message they couldn't locate, an account of Abby's whereabouts that changed, and a description of Andrew's return that did not match the body.

The note was central to Lizzie's explanation. According to her, a messenger had brought Abby a handwritten request at the front door to attend a friend who had fallen ill. Lizzie said she had told Bridget about it. When Andrew came home and asked where his wife was, she had given him the same answer: Abby had gone to visit the sick friend.

Marshal Hilliard and his officers searched the house for the paper, looking from cellar to attic and checking tables, mantels, and waste receptacles where it might have been left or discarded. They found nothing. Lizzie suggested that Abby might have burned it. Outside the house, inquiries among acquaintances and local households were no more successful. No friend came forward to say she had sent for Abby, and no messenger was identified.

After Andrew's body was discovered, Lizzie offered a different possibility. Speaking to Bridget and Mrs. Churchill, she suggested that Abby was upstairs. She said she thought she had heard her stepmother return and encouraged someone to look for her. The women went up and found Abby in the guest room.

Lizzie's stated reason for the change was that she believed Abby had come back. To investigators, it was suspicious that she first accounted for her stepmother's absence and then proposed searching the floor where she lay dead. Still, asking for an upstairs search was not the same as identifying the body's precise location beside the bed. The unconfirmed note and the change in Lizzie's account gave police reasons to question her, without resolving what she had known.

Another discrepancy concerned Andrew's footwear. In the account recorded by investigators, Lizzie said she had helped her father remove his outdoor boots and that he had put on slippers before resting. Some accounts also record Bridget recalling mention of slippers. Yet the body photographs show Andrew still wearing his heavy, lace-up leather walking boots on the sofa. They remained on his feet, laced, and there was no discarded pair beside him to match the action she had described. No slippers lay on the floor nearby. To police, the slippers story looked like an invented domestic detail, intended to show Andrew resting peacefully before Lizzie left for the barn.

Attention also turned to Lizzie's clothing. When neighbors and officers saw her after the alarm, they reported no visible blood on her face, hands, or hair. The dress she wore appeared clean. Bridget remembered that Lizzie had come downstairs in a blue dress earlier that morning. In a house where the attacks had spattered blood over furniture and walls, officers wanted to know whether the clothing they saw was what she had worn throughout the morning.

Their first inspection of the house had been cursory. On the day of the murders, police did not seize Lizzie's wardrobe or closely examine all her personal effects. The question of her clothing would acquire greater urgency three days later, through something a friend witnessed in the kitchen.

On Sunday, August 7, Alice Russell was staying at Second Street to help the sisters. Emma had returned from Fairhaven, and Alice was sharing the house with them through the immediate aftermath of the deaths. At about eleven that morning, she entered the kitchen and found Lizzie beside the stove holding a dress.

Alice recognized it as an everyday morning gown Lizzie had worn earlier in the spring. It was cotton Bedford cord, light blue with small dark figures. Lizzie was tearing the material into strips to feed into the fire. When Alice asked what she was doing, Lizzie said she was going to burn the old thing because it was covered with paint.

Emma was present and supported the explanation. She said she had already advised her sister to get rid of the dress because it was no longer fit to wear. As the sisters described it, the garment was a spoiled piece of clothing that had outlived its usefulness. An ordinary household disposal, they said, had brought Lizzie to the stove.

Alice saw the act in the circumstances surrounding the house. Police were stationed outside. The neighborhood was watching, and almost anything the sisters did could be treated as significant. A garment that might otherwise have been thrown away without comment was now being destroyed while officers investigated two murders committed at close range.

She warned her friend: "If I were you I wouldn't let anybody see me do that, Lizzie."

Lizzie offered no argument, but she did not stop. She fed the torn strips into the stove until the gown had been consumed. Watching from across the kitchen, Alice could describe the dress she recognized and the explanation the sisters gave. She could also describe the warning she had felt compelled to make while watching it disappear.

The event would divide people who otherwise agreed about what Alice had seen. To those already suspicious of Lizzie, the timing looked like the destruction of evidence. To those who accepted the sisters' explanation, it was an ill-judged decision to dispose of an old dress while the household was under scrutiny. The same action could be made part of either account, but the fabric itself was no longer available.

Bridget's memory of blue clothing on Thursday and Alice's description of a light blue gown on Sunday invited comparison. Nobody could establish whether the burned dress was the one Lizzie had worn on the morning of August 4 or an old garment ruined by paint, as the sisters said. Once it went into the stove, its stains could never be examined. What remained were the witnesses' recollections and Lizzie's explanation. In the coming days, the authorities would require her to give her account of the morning again, this time under oath.

[[ACT:act5]]

For a week after the murders, Lizzie was not under arrest. She remained at Second Street with Emma and Alice Russell. Outside, police watched the property while reporters, curious neighbors, and other onlookers gathered nearby. Inside, the sisters continued living in the rooms where their father and stepmother had been killed. Daily necessities went on around the investigation: meals in the kitchen, movement through the downstairs rooms, nights in the upstairs bedrooms.

The police were narrowing their attention. On Saturday evening, August 6, Mayor John Coughlin visited the house with the lead investigator. They told Lizzie directly that she was considered a suspect in the deaths. No arrest followed that night. She remained at home, but the officials had removed any doubt about the direction of their inquiry.

Three days later, they began a proceeding in which witnesses could be required to answer questions under oath. District Attorney Hosea Knowlton and local officials had consulted in Marshal Hilliard's office before moving upstairs for the hearing.

The doors were closed to reporters and spectators. Witnesses entered individually, with Bridget among the first called. This was an investigative hearing, not a trial before a jury. The questions and answers would create a sworn record, and Knowlton could return witnesses to details that had been uncertain or disputed in their earlier statements.

When Lizzie was called, her attorney could not accompany her. Andrew Jackson Jennings, the family's lawyer, was barred from the room under the procedure used for the inquest. Lizzie sat before the district attorney and magistrate without counsel to advise her, object to a question, or help clarify an answer. She had already been told she was suspected, but she faced the examination alone.

There was another unusual circumstance. After the murders, Dr. Bowen had prescribed morphine to settle Lizzie's nerves. According to the accounts of her treatment, she had taken regular doses during the days before the hearing and was under the medication's influence when she gave her evidence. The questioning continued across three days, from August 9 through August 11. It would be the only occasion on which she gave a sworn account from the witness stand of the events of August 4.

Knowlton asked where she had been when Andrew returned. Lizzie first placed herself on the stairs, then in the kitchen. At another point, she was outside in the yard eating pears. Asked to retrace familiar movements through the house, she did not give one consistent sequence. The differences concerned the time when her father came through the front entrance and moved toward the sitting room.

The purpose of her barn visit also changed. In her initial account to police, she had described looking for iron or tin to repair a window screen. At the inquest, she said she had been searching for lead sinkers for a planned fishing trip with Emma in Fairhaven. When Knowlton pressed her about spending roughly 20 minutes in the hot loft, she described lingering there to eat pears.

The questioning returned to where she had gone upstairs, what sounds she had heard, and where she stood while Bridget struggled with the front door. Knowlton set her statements against one another. When confronted with the discrepancies, Lizzie answered, "I don't know what I have said."

At other moments, she challenged the questions themselves. "Some of your questions I have difficulty answering because I don't know just how you mean them."

The exchanges preserved in the transcript were sometimes hesitant and sometimes confrontational. The examiner sought firm distinctions; Lizzie changed an answer, resisted his wording, or could not account for what she had said before. The closed hearing produced not the settled chronology officials wanted, but further contradictions.

Officials described her behavior as erratic at times and interpreted the changes as evasions. The transcript cannot establish whether those answers came from deception, grief, morphine, or confusion under questioning without counsel. Those circumstances complicate the investigators' reading without making the answers consistent. Knowlton and Hilliard nevertheless regarded the sworn account as strengthening their suspicions.

On the afternoon of August 11, as the third day of the inquest ended, Marshal Hilliard executed a warrant for Lizzie's arrest. One week after Andrew and Abby were killed, she was taken into custody and charged with their murders. Charged, not convicted. The accusation would now proceed through the courts rather than through questioning at the family home.

Lizzie appeared before the district court and pleaded not guilty. She was transferred to the county jail in Taunton, 20 miles north of Fall River, where she would remain while the prosecution sought to bring the case to trial. The move ended the week she had spent at Second Street under observation. Emma could return to the house; Lizzie could not.

Nearly four months passed before the grand jury made its decision. The case remained entirely circumstantial. Investigators had neither a confession nor an eyewitness to the killings, and no murder weapon had been scientifically established. Lizzie was confined while the state sought enough evidence to bring its accusation before a trial jury.

At the beginning of December, Alice Russell testified about the destruction of the dress. The friend who had stayed to support the sisters now described under oath how Lizzie had torn the light blue Bedford cord gown into strips and fed them into the kitchen stove on the Sunday after the murders. Her evidence supplied the grand jury with something police had not witnessed during their first searches.

Alice's testimony persuaded the grand jury that the destruction of potential evidence warranted a trial. On December 2, 1892, it returned an indictment charging Lizzie with murder. The case went forward to the Superior Court, with the trial set for June 5, 1893, in New Bedford. What Alice had seen in the kitchen would be part of the accusation presented there.

Through the winter and spring, Lizzie remained in Taunton while the lawyers prepared. Knowlton had the three-day inquest transcript, with its changes of location, its different explanations for the barn visit, and the exchanges in which he had challenged her answers. For the prosecution, those were sworn words from the person it accused.

Jennings had been excluded from the room where they were obtained. At trial, he would have the chance to challenge their use. Before a jury could weigh Lizzie's contradictory testimony, the court would have to decide whether the jurors would be allowed to hear it at all.

[[ACT:act6]]

Lizzie Borden's trial opened in New Bedford, 20 miles south of Fall River, on June 5, 1893. Ten months had passed since her arrest. Spectators filled the gallery and gathered outside for a glimpse of the Sunday school teacher accused of killing her father and stepmother.

Three Superior Court justices presided over a jury of 12 men. Fall River residents had been excluded from the panel to reduce local bias, so the jurors came from elsewhere in Bristol County. Their knowledge of the household would have to come through the witnesses and exhibits admitted in court.

Opposite them, Andrew Jennings was joined by George D. Robinson, a former governor of Massachusetts. The family's wealth had secured a defense team with considerable experience and public standing. Robinson brought a commanding courtroom presence, while Jennings had represented Lizzie since the investigation and knew the circumstances in which her statements had been taken.

The state sought a capital conviction. The defense did not have to identify another killer; it had to show that the accusation against Lizzie had not been proved. Some of the most consequential disputes would therefore take place before the judges, over evidence the prosecution wanted the jury to hear.

Lizzie sat beside her counsel in black, carrying a lace handkerchief and smelling salts. For much of the proceedings she maintained the quiet composure police had remarked on after the deaths. But the trial brought the physical reality of the attacks into the courtroom. The prosecution introduced the victims' skulls, removed and cleaned during autopsies so the injuries could be demonstrated.

At the sight of her father's shattered skull, Lizzie fainted in her chair. Attendants revived her before the proceedings continued. The remains were being presented as evidence in the same room where she faced the possibility of a death sentence for causing those injuries.

The defense's most important challenge concerned the inquest transcript. Robinson and Jennings argued that Lizzie's sworn statements had been improperly obtained: she had been questioned for hours without legal counsel and while taking prescribed morphine. The judges excluded the record. The absence of a lawyer during the inquest became a decisive barrier to its use, and the jury never heard the conflicting sworn answers that had helped persuade authorities to arrest her.

Lizzie did not take the witness stand at trial. She exercised her right to remain silent and left her lawyers to conduct the defense. Knowlton therefore had no opportunity to cross-examine her about the morning or demand that she reconcile her inquest answers before the jurors.

The court also excluded the prussic acid testimony. Drugstore clerk Eli Bence was the witness through whom Knowlton hoped to introduce the alleged attempt to buy poison on August 3. The prosecution wanted the episode treated as evidence of premeditation, an earlier effort to obtain a lethal substance.

The defense objected that neither victim had died of poison. It argued that the disputed drugstore exchange had no proper connection to deaths caused by blows. The judges sustained the objection. The jury did not hear Bence's account, and the prosecution could not use the alleged purchase attempt to establish prior intent.

The case the jurors heard was therefore narrower than the collection of suspicions surrounding Lizzie's arrest. Knowlton still had witnesses who could describe the household, the morning's events, and Lizzie's conduct afterward. One of them was Bridget Sullivan, whose testimony returned the courtroom to the locked front door.

Bridget stood by her account that she had heard Lizzie laugh from the upstairs landing as Andrew waited to enter. She also explained why she believed someone above her would have laughed. Frustrated by the stubborn lock, she had uttered a curse. In Bridget's understanding, Lizzie was amused by that slip of profanity.

That explanation gave the sound an ordinary cause. Bridget was not claiming that laughter itself revealed knowledge of a crime. But she remained certain about where it came from. Her account still placed Lizzie above the front hall, near the guest room. The defense maintained that Lizzie had not laughed and had been downstairs. The contradiction that began at the doorway remained unresolved in court.

To establish motive, Knowlton called witnesses who described hostility toward Abby. Acquaintances and a dressmaker testified that Lizzie had spoken of an unhappy home, refused to regard Abby as her mother, and said she hated her. The prosecution used those statements to challenge the image of an entirely harmonious, respectable family.

The defense emphasized Lizzie's churchgoing life and disputed the implication that resentment had led to murder. The jury heard competing portraits of the same woman: a devout daughter whose public conduct had been conventional, and a person witnesses said had expressed deep bitterness toward her stepmother. The state's argument required the jurors to connect that hostility with the violence.

Alice Russell's testimony concerned conduct rather than a reported feeling. The prosecution presented her account of the dress as evidence of deliberate destruction. Her warning to Lizzie mattered because it came from a friend who had been staying in the house to help. Even someone close to the sisters had recognized how the act would look.

Knowlton asked the jury to interpret it as concealment. But the state did not substantiate its claim that the gown was the one worn during the murders. The prosecution could argue from the destruction; it could not demonstrate the identification on which its strongest interpretation depended.

Emma took the stand for her sister. She said disposing of the gown had been her own suggestion and described it as old, faded, and soiled with paint before the deaths. The defense also called a painter, who confirmed that paint had gotten onto a dress during work at the property. That testimony gave support to the explanation Lizzie had offered in the kitchen.

Robinson pressed the circumstances. If Lizzie had been a calculating killer trying to conceal a bloody garment, he argued, why destroy it so openly while police were stationed just outside? The prosecution saw a purposeful attempt to eliminate evidence. The defense saw an act so public that it was inconsistent with the secret disposal the state proposed.

Neither side disputed Alice's presence or the essential act she had witnessed. They asked the jurors to draw opposite conclusions from it. Her testimony could be accepted while the prosecution's interpretation was rejected. Emma's testimony, the painter's account, and Robinson's argument all addressed the reason for the destruction rather than denying that it happened.

On June 20, before the case went to the jury, Lizzie was given an opportunity to address the court. She rose and said, "I am innocent. I leave it to my counsel to speak for me."

Justice Justin Dewey then instructed the jurors on the standard they had to apply. Suspicion, probability, and conjecture were not enough. The Commonwealth had to establish its accusation beyond a reasonable doubt. The men were not being asked simply whether some of Lizzie's behavior seemed troubling or whether the household gave rise to suspicion. They had to decide whether the state had proved that she committed the murders.

After two weeks of testimony and argument, the decision came quickly. Most accounts put the deliberation at about 90 minutes; others describe a shorter interval. Some reports suggested that agreement had come early and that the jurors waited before returning so they would not appear indecently hasty. Whatever the precise interval, there was no prolonged debate lasting into another day.

At half past three, word spread through the courthouse that the jury had agreed. Spectators pressed into the gallery. The men returned to their places, and the clerk asked whether they found the prisoner guilty or not guilty of the murders of Andrew and Abby Borden.

The foreman's answer was unanimous: "Not guilty."

Lizzie bent forward, buried her face in her hands, and sobbed. Cheers, shouts, and applause broke out across the gallery, loud enough to carry to the crowd outside. The courtroom that had displayed her parents' remains now filled with an unmistakable public expression of relief at her acquittal.

The prosecution had presented opportunity, alleged motive, and behavior it considered suspicious. It had not supplied an eyewitness to the killings, a confession, or a conclusive connection between Lizzie and the acts themselves. Its circumstantial argument had not removed reasonable doubt.

A verdict of not guilty was not proof of innocence, and it was not proof that anyone else had committed the crimes. It meant that the prosecution had not proved its charges under the law.

For Lizzie, the consequence was final. The protection against double jeopardy meant Massachusetts could not try her again for Andrew and Abby's murders. Chief Justice Albert Mason discharged her, ending the confinement that had begun the previous August. The capital charge no longer hung over her.

Emma, Jennings, and Robinson were there as Lizzie left the defense table. She passed the spectator benches and made her way out of the courthouse into the June afternoon. She would not be returning to the county jail in Taunton. After ten months in custody, Lizzie Borden walked free.

The public story had already traveled far beyond the evidence. On October 10, 1892, while Lizzie was still awaiting trial, the Boston Globe published a front-page article by Henry Trickey claiming that she had been pregnant and that Andrew had threatened to evict her. It supplied readers with a dramatic private conflict that seemed to explain the crimes.

The story was fabricated. This was not a disputed recollection or a contested interpretation of something found at Second Street. A major newspaper had presented an invented account as news while the woman it concerned was jailed awaiting trial.

A more durable distortion entered American folklore through the familiar rhyme. Its rhythm made it easier to remember than testimony or medical findings. But the weapon was a hatchet, Abby was Lizzie's stepmother, and the number of blows was far below the rhyme's totals.

Most consequentially, the verse named the killer without qualification. The accusation became part of a chant repeated by children and printed in newspapers, until it sounded as settled as a childhood fact.

After the trial, she left Fall River and eventually settled in Butte, Montana, far from the public attention surrounding the Borden family. She lived there until her death in 1948. Later accounts alleged that, before she died, she had told her sister she had lied or altered parts of her testimony to help Lizzie.

No written confession, sworn account, or contemporaneous record supports that story. It surfaced as secondhand rumor, not as a documented correction Bridget made to her evidence. During her lifetime, she did not publicly withdraw the account she had given in court. The alleged deathbed admission cannot settle the disagreement about the stairs or establish what she knew beyond her testimony.

Lizzie remained the principal focus of public suspicion after her acquittal, but no one else was charged. Other names and explanations continued to circulate, sometimes beginning with a genuine connection to the household and sometimes depending on a relationship or event that could not be established.

John Morse was an early person for police to scrutinize. He had arrived unexpectedly the day before the murders, discussed property with Andrew, and slept in the room where Abby died. His presence seemed especially striking because he did not often visit. The guest room linked him directly to the place of the first killing.

But his movements after leaving that morning were corroborated. Morse gave detailed information about streetcars, numbers on transit workers' caps, and people he had encountered. Outside witnesses supported his account of visiting relatives across town. Police dismissed him as a suspect. His overnight stay explained why Abby had a bed to change; it did not overcome the evidence of where he went afterward.

Later theories proposed an outsider: an unknown intruder, a resentful tenant, or someone with a grudge against Andrew's business practices. Lizzie's warning to Alice that her father might have enemies gave that idea a place in the story. Yet no identified outsider was connected to the attacks, and no witness supplied the missing person's arrival or departure.

More speculative accounts implicated Emma or Bridget, proposed paternal abuse as a cause, or named a supposed illegitimate son, William Borden. Records discredited the claim that William was Andrew's son. None of these proposed explanations acquired contemporary documentation, physical evidence, or verifiable testimony establishing who committed the murders.

For Lizzie and Emma, the deaths also brought an immediate change in material circumstances. They inherited from an estate valued at about $300,000. The father who had determined their household's austere way of life was gone, and the sisters now had the resources to choose a different one.

They left Second Street for The Hill, the neighborhood Andrew had declined to join. There they bought a spacious, modern Victorian house with 14 rooms and named it Maplecroft. The new home provided the comfort and space absent from their childhood house. They could leave behind the bedrooms divided by bolted doors and the downstairs rooms through which their daily movements had passed.

Leaving the address did not restore Lizzie's former standing. Fall River largely shunned her. Old acquaintances avoided her, some crossing the street rather than meeting her. Shopkeepers served her in cold silence. The community in which she had taught Sunday school and worked on charitable committees no longer offered the same familiar welcome.

She spent much of her remaining life secluded at Maplecroft, taking private carriage rides and occasional quiet trips out of town. Wealth gave her comfort and a measure of distance from public attention, but it could not make neighbors forget the accusation. The cheers in the courthouse were not the reception she found at home.

Emma remained with her until 1905. Then a dispute neither sister publicly explained ended their shared household. Emma packed her belongings, moved out of Maplecroft, and left Fall River permanently. The sisters never saw or spoke to one another again.

For most of Lizzie's life, Emma had been the older sister who looked after her. She had returned from Fairhaven after the deaths, stayed beside her through the accusation, and testified in her defense. Their separation removed that lifelong presence from Lizzie's daily life. Neither woman left a public explanation that resolves why they parted.

Lizzie lived at Maplecroft until June 1, 1927, when she died of pneumonia at 66. Emma died nine days later. Both were brought back to Fall River and buried in the Borden family plot at Oak Grove Cemetery, near Andrew and Abby. The sisters who had left Second Street eventually returned to the same family ground.

The wooden house outlasted all of them. Today, it operates as a museum and historic inn. Visitors can enter the sitting room, climb the front staircase, and stand in the guest bedroom. The narrow routes through the house remain tangible, even where the record of what happened along them stops.

Abby is less often remembered as a person than as one of the bodies in those rooms. She had spent nearly three decades tending the household's ordinary needs, living amid relationships other people later described in sharply different ways. The arguments about money, the trial, and the rhyme gave her a narrow place in the story of her own death.

Before any of that, on the morning of August 4, she had a guest room to put in order.

At around nine, Abby went up the front stairs to change the bed John Morse had slept in. The breakfast dishes had been cleared, and the guest room overlooked the quiet street below. She carried clean linens into the room and set about the familiar work of making it ready again: smoothing the coverlet, adjusting the pillow shams, and dusting the furniture. Andrew had gone to conduct his business. Bridget had windows to wash. Abby was upstairs, attending to a room another member of the family had just used.

She never walked back down. Someone reached the guest room while she was there.
