[[ACT:act1]]

At about half past ten on the morning of August 4, 1892, Andrew Borden returned to his house on Second Street in Fall River, Massachusetts. He tried his key in the front door, but the lock would not give. Inside, the family’s maid, Bridget Sullivan, heard him at the entrance and came to let him in. As she struggled with the lock, she heard laughter from above her, at the top of the front staircase. Bridget said it was Andrew’s younger daughter, Lizzie. The landing was just outside the guest bedroom.

Lizzie denied it. She said she had not laughed and had not been standing on the upstairs landing. When her father came home, she insisted, she was downstairs. It was a disagreement about a few moments in an otherwise ordinary morning: a father returning from town, a maid opening a stubborn door, a daughter somewhere inside the house. But before the morning was over, the position of each person would matter. Bridget placed Lizzie at the head of the front stairs. Lizzie placed herself on the ground floor.

A little after eleven, Bridget was in her third-floor bedroom, resting after washing windows, when Lizzie called up to her: “Come down quickly. Father is dead. Someone came in and killed him.” Bridget hurried downstairs and found Lizzie near the back entrance. Next door, Adelaide Churchill noticed Lizzie at the doorway and called across to ask what was wrong. “Oh, Mrs. Churchill, do come over,” Lizzie answered. “Someone has killed father.” Mrs. Churchill crossed the yard and entered the house. The alarm had brought people to the back door, but the front staircase would soon draw their attention again.

In the downstairs sitting room, 70-year-old Andrew Borden lay stretched out on the sofa. His position might at first have suggested that he was resting after his walk downtown. His injuries left no doubt about what had happened. Someone had struck him repeatedly in the face and head with a hatchet, fracturing his skull and damaging his features almost beyond recognition. Blood had spattered the cushions and the wall behind him. The attack had taken place inside his own sitting room, in daylight, while other members of the household were close by.

Attention turned to Andrew’s wife, Abby. Bridget and Mrs. Churchill went to look for her upstairs. They approached by the front staircase, the same stairs from which Bridget said she had heard Lizzie laughing when Andrew came home. At the top was the guest bedroom. Between the bed and the dressing bureau, 64-year-old Abby Borden lay face down on the carpet in a pool of blood. She, too, had been struck repeatedly with a sharp, heavy weapon. What had begun as a call for help for Andrew was now the discovery of two killings, on different floors of the house.

Outside was Fall River, a prosperous textile city on Mount Hope Bay. The Borden house stood at 92 Second Street, only a block from the busy commercial center. This was not an isolated property where someone could approach across empty land. It was a modest wooden home among the streets and businesses of the city. The family routinely kept its exterior doors locked. Yet someone had attacked two people inside it that Thursday morning, without any witness reporting that they had seen the violence or recognized the person responsible.

Lizzie’s older sister, Emma, was away visiting friends in Fairhaven, 15 miles east of Fall River. During the killings, the only people known to be in the house besides Andrew and Abby were 32-year-old Lizzie and 26-year-old Bridget. Neither said she had seen an attacker. The doors and windows showed no sign of forced entry, and the rooms had not been ransacked for valuables. There was no obvious trail of a burglary interrupted or a robbery carried out. The daughter and the maid had survived; the father and stepmother were dead.

Shortly after a quarter past eleven, a telephone call from a neighboring stable reached the police department, reporting that Andrew Borden had been killed. Marshal Rufus Hilliard received the alert. The department was shorthanded that morning because many officers were away at their annual picnic at Rocky Point. The men still on duty had to make the first response. Officer George Allen was dispatched to Second Street and arrived at about twenty past eleven, as word of the deaths was spreading through the neighborhood.

Neighbors were already gathering near the property. Allen entered a home in which people seeking to help had arrived before the police, and where there were now two rooms to examine. More officers followed. They had to establish who had been present, when the victims had last been seen, and how someone could have moved through the house without being noticed. The first accounts would come from the people already there: Lizzie, Bridget, and the neighbors who had answered the alarm. Among those accounts was the disagreement about the front staircase.

Andrew’s return had been a fixed event in both women’s recollections. Bridget had let him in. Lizzie had been somewhere nearby. But the two women did not agree about where. That difference would follow them through questioning and eventually into court, alongside a much larger problem: two people had been killed in a locked household, and no one admitted seeing who had done it. No one was ever convicted, and the murders of Andrew and Abby Borden have never been officially solved.

[[ACT:act2]]

Lizzie Andrew Borden was born in Fall River on July 19, 1860. In the summer of 1892, she had just turned 32. She was unmarried and still lived with her father, but her life extended beyond the house on Second Street. At Central Congregational Church, she taught Sunday school, joined religious societies, and took part in charitable work through church and hospital committees. People knew her as a devout woman from an established family. The accusation that would later be brought against her was difficult to reconcile with the familiar figure they had seen at church.

Her sister Emma was 41, nine years older, and also unmarried. Reserved and quiet, Emma had looked out for Lizzie since their mother, Sarah, died when Lizzie was a child. The sisters shared a home and a dependence on their father, though by August 4 Emma was visiting friends in Fairhaven. The woman who had long helped care for her younger sister was not there during the morning that would divide their lives into before and after. She would learn of the deaths away from Second Street.

Their father, Andrew Jackson Borden, had built a considerable fortune. He began as a furniture maker, producing furniture and caskets, and moved into real estate, textile investments, and banking. By 1892, he was president of the Union Savings Bank and one of Fall River’s wealthiest men. His success did not translate into an expansive household. While other prosperous families lived in large homes in the fashionable district called The Hill, Andrew kept his family downtown in the wooden house on Second Street. It lacked modern conveniences, including indoor plumbing. His reputation for frugality was as well established as his wealth.

Abby Durfee Gray had married Andrew in June 1865, about two years after Sarah’s death. She had been his wife for 27 years by the summer of the murders. At 64, she had spent much of her adult life tending the household in which Lizzie and Emma had grown up. She was the only maternal presence Lizzie could remember from most of her childhood, but that did not mean Lizzie regarded her as a mother. Abby occupied a place in the family that was longstanding and, in some accounts, persistently uncomfortable.

Money was one source of disagreement. Reports published after the murders described a dispute around 1887, when Andrew bought a house for Abby’s half-sister to live in. According to those reports, Lizzie and Emma objected to family money going to Abby’s relatives and wanted equal consideration for themselves. The story became a familiar explanation for hostility in the house, though the surviving support for the reported feud is weak. It should not be treated as a fully documented account of what the sisters said or how deeply the quarrel affected them.

There was, however, a concrete property arrangement. Andrew transferred rental houses to his daughters for $1 each. Several years later, he bought the properties back for $5,000. The sisters gained money of their own, but neither left the family home. Andrew continued to control the household’s standard of living, and the question of how his fortune might eventually be divided gave accounts of the family tension their force. In the more hostile descriptions, the daughters feared that Abby’s relatives would benefit at their expense. Those descriptions would later help the prosecution propose a motive.

Accounts of the women’s daily relations do not agree. Some acquaintances described coldness and separate meals, with Lizzie and Emma avoiding the table where Andrew and Abby ate. Others, including Lizzie in her formal testimony, described ordinary, cordial exchanges and denied open conflict. One documented statement is more definite than these competing portraits. When an officer spoke to Lizzie about her mother on the day of the murders, Lizzie corrected him: “She is not my mother, sir. She is my father’s wife. My mother died when I was a child.” It establishes how she defined the relationship, not everything she felt about it.

The house itself made separation easy in some ways and movement awkward in others. Built originally for more than one family, it had no upstairs hallway joining the front and rear sections. Rooms opened into other rooms, and interior doors were often fastened. A doorway connected Lizzie’s bedroom with Andrew and Abby’s room, but it was bolted on both sides. To reach her father’s room without opening that barrier, Lizzie had to go down the front stairs, cross the ground floor through the sitting room and dining room, and climb the back stairs from the kitchen.

The front staircase served Lizzie’s room and the guest bedroom. The back stairs led to Andrew and Abby’s room and toward the servants’ quarters beneath the roof. Bridget Sullivan slept on the third floor. She was an Irish domestic servant, 26 years old, who had worked for the Bordens for three years. Cleaning, washing windows, and preparing the household’s meals carried her through the shared rooms downstairs. In this arrangement, being upstairs did not mean having a simple route to every other upstairs room. A person’s choice of staircase mattered.

On Wednesday, August 3, another person joined the household. John Vinnicum Morse arrived unexpectedly that afternoon. He was Sarah Borden’s brother, making him Lizzie and Emma’s maternal uncle, and had spent much of his adult life farming in Iowa. He had come east on personal business and did not often visit the family. Andrew invited his former brother-in-law to stay for several days while they discussed property and investments. Morse was given the guest bedroom at the top of the front stairs, the room Abby would go to straighten the following morning.

The household had already been unsettled by sickness. Andrew, Abby, and Bridget had suffered severe stomach distress during the days before August 4. Some feared poisoning. The apparent explanation was mutton that had been left out in the summer heat and eaten over several days, but the illness fed a sense that something was wrong. Andrew himself suspected that someone might have tried to poison the family. Whatever caused the sickness, Bridget was still feeling its effects when she went about her work on the morning of the murders.

That Wednesday evening, between seven and nine, Lizzie visited her friend Alice Russell. Alice later described her as anxious and depressed. Lizzie spoke of an unknown threat, a feeling that something terrible was going to happen, though she could not say what or when. She mentioned her father’s business dealings and suggested that he had enemies who might harm him. It was a conversation about fear without an identified danger. Alice heard it from a friend living in a tense household where several people had recently been ill.

A separate account placed Lizzie in a drugstore earlier that day. A clerk reported that a woman matching her description had asked to buy prussic acid, or hydrogen cyanide, a deadly poison. Refused a sale without a prescription, the woman said she wanted it to clean a sealskin cape. The clerk again refused, and other pharmacy staff supported his account of the exchange. Lizzie denied visiting the shop, and her attorneys maintained that another woman had been mistaken for her. No purchase was completed. The identification remained disputed, leaving an allegation about the day before the murders that neither side could conclusively settle.

[[ACT:act3]]

The household began to disperse soon after breakfast on Thursday, August 4. Accounts differ about who ate together at around seven. Some place Andrew, Abby, John Morse, and Bridget at the table while Lizzie remained upstairs; others place Lizzie with her parents or eating later by herself. The departures are clearer. Morse left at about a quarter to nine to visit relatives across town and expected to return around noon. Andrew went out at about nine to attend to business at the banks and in the commercial district. Abby, Lizzie, and Bridget remained at the property.

Around nine, Abby went up the front stairs to straighten the guest bedroom and change the bed Morse had used. This is the last activity for which the accounts confirm her alive. Downstairs, Lizzie told Bridget about a sale on dress goods at a local store and suggested that she might like to go. Bridget, still unwell from the household’s stomach sickness, declined. She stayed to wash the ground-floor windows. Andrew returned at about half past ten and found the front door locked. Bridget admitted him. By the time the doctors and police examined the house, both he and Abby were dead.

The injuries showed that each victim had been attacked at close range with a sharp, heavy blade. Andrew had been struck across the head and face as he lay on the sofa. The blows fractured his skull and facial bones; one cut through an eye. Abby’s wounds were concentrated at the back of her head and neck, following a blow near the side of her face. There were no signs that either had raised a hand successfully to resist. Their positions and injuries gave the examiners evidence of the attacks, even though no one had reported witnessing them.

The exact number of blows is not consistently recorded. The BBC’s account gives 11 for Andrew; Legends of America gives ten, the number also recorded as head wounds by medical examiner Dr. William Dolan. For Abby, accounts generally give 18 or 19; Dolan recorded 18 head wounds and a wound to her back. These differences do not change the nature of the killings, but they matter when later versions turn estimates into fixed, memorable numbers. The medical descriptions record repeated hatchet strikes, not the much larger totals that would eventually enter folklore.

More important to the investigation was the condition of the bodies. Upstairs, Abby was cold to the touch. Her blood was dark, coagulated, and dry on the carpet beneath her. Downstairs, Andrew’s body was still warm. His wounds were fresh, and liquid blood continued to ooze onto the sofa and floor. The contrast established the order of the deaths. Abby had been killed first, and her body had remained in the guest room while ordinary activity continued elsewhere in the house. Andrew’s death belonged to a later part of the morning.

The doctors could estimate the interval, but not set it to a precise number of minutes. One coroner’s estimate placed Abby’s death between nine and half past ten, while several assessments favored about nine-thirty. Andrew’s death was placed between ten-thirty and eleven-ten. Medical opinions described a gap of roughly an hour to an hour and a half, with some allowing nearly two hours. These were estimates drawn from the bodies and the available accounts, not exact measurements. There was no scientific doubt about the essential sequence: the two people had been killed some time apart.

That sequence changed the scale of the inquiry. This was not a single attack in which one victim had come to the aid of the other. There had been time between the deaths for Andrew to continue his business downtown, return to the locked house, and lie down in the sitting room. During part of that interval, Abby was already upstairs. The police needed to account not only for the moment of Andrew’s death but for a longer stretch of the morning, during which the first attack had not brought anyone running to the guest room.

In the cellar, officers searched among coal bins and stored tools for something capable of making the wounds. They found axes and intact hatchets. Another item attracted particular attention: a hatchet head in a box of dusty tools, with only a short piece of wood remaining in its eye where the handle had broken. Several officers thought the break looked fresh. This became the handleless hatchet, the implement the prosecution would argue had been used on both victims. Its blade was roughly three and a half inches across, a dimension the state said was consistent with the damage to the skulls.

The prosecution’s explanation went beyond the shape of the blade. It proposed that the killer had broken off the handle because the wood would have absorbed blood, then covered the metal head in ash or fine cellar dust to conceal recent use. The broken handle and the coating were therefore meant to be signs of concealment, not simply the condition of an old tool. If that interpretation held, an object lying among the household’s discarded equipment might connect the cellar with both rooms where the victims had been found.

But the officers did not agree about what they had seen. One contradicted the claim of a fresh break, saying the wood did not show evidence of a recent snap. The defense also introduced testimony about the thick accumulation of ash in the cellar. A large ash pile had coated the surroundings, so a dusty hatchet head was consistent with an implement left there for months. Chemical testing did not establish conclusive blood on its blade. The dispute was not just over what the object meant; it began with conflicting descriptions of the object itself. The tool remained a proposed weapon, not an identified one.

Another cellar discovery seemed, at first, more directly connected to blood. In the washroom, police found a pail containing soaked pieces of cloth. Lizzie told them the cloths were menstrual rags, unrelated to the killings. Her physician, Dr. Seabury Bowen, confirmed that she was menstruating. Investigative accounts describe the officers as accepting the explanation and leaving the cloths rather than seizing them for examination. The surviving descriptions differ in how fully the discovery and its handling were recorded, so the episode cannot support a more precise reconstruction of what every officer inspected or decided.

The point is narrower than later speculation sometimes makes it. The pail was noticed, Lizzie gave a domestic explanation, and her doctor supported it. According to those accounts, no chemical testing was done to establish whether the blood might have come from a victim. The police were working before blood typing and without modern methods for identifying minute traces. Those limits affected what they could determine, but they do not turn the rags into evidence of a cleanup. The investigation left them without a verified connection to either death.

Elsewhere, searches produced no hidden blood-drenched garment and no missing money or jewelry suggesting robbery. There were no marks of forced entry. A search of the barn found no footprints in its dust. These observations helped officers test accounts of the morning, but none identified a killer. Even the apparently promising cellar discovery had to pass through disputed observations about wood, ash, and the absence of conclusive blood. The violence had left extensive damage to two people and the rooms around them, yet had not supplied an object that could be placed securely in a particular person’s hands.

More than an hour separated the killings. Lizzie and Bridget were the people known to have remained at the property through the critical part of the morning; Morse had been there earlier, before leaving to make his visit. The physical examination established that Abby died before Andrew and that both had been struck repeatedly with a sharp, heavy weapon. It could not tie those acts to a person. With the bodies examined and the tools collected, the officers had to return to what people said they had done: which rooms they had entered, when they had left, and what they claimed to have noticed.

Lizzie gave police an account of a morning in which she had noticed nothing dangerous until she found her father. Abby, she said, had gone out after receiving a note asking her to visit a sick friend. Bridget had been washing windows. Andrew had returned from downtown and settled on the sitting-room sofa to rest. With her father lying down and her stepmother supposedly away, Lizzie had gone into the yard and then to the barn. The attack on Andrew, in her account, took place during an absence of about 20 minutes.

She said she had gone to the barn loft to find a piece of iron for repairing a window screen. On returning through the screen door shortly after eleven, she entered the sitting room and saw Andrew dead on the couch. Her explanation left a short opportunity for someone else to enter, attack him, and get away while she was outside. It also depended on Abby’s absence. When Andrew had asked where his wife was after coming home, Lizzie said she had told him about the note and the sick friend. Bridget had heard the same explanation.

Several officers were struck by Lizzie’s composure during questioning. They described her as calm, controlled, and direct, without the tears or outbursts they expected after such a discovery. To them, that restraint seemed unnatural. Other observers understood it as shock or an effort to preserve dignity. The observation belonged to the people judging her behavior, not to a medical finding about how grief should appear. It nonetheless influenced the officers’ approach. As they checked the details of her story, they were already paying attention to the way she answered as well as to the answers themselves.

The note gave them something specific to investigate. According to Lizzie, a messenger had brought a written request for Abby to attend a friend who had fallen ill. Police looked through the house for it, checking places where a paper might have been left or discarded. They found none. Lizzie suggested that Abby might have burned it. Inquiries outside the house were no more successful: no friend came forward to say she had sent for Abby, and no messenger was identified. The errand was described in Lizzie’s account, but no independent person or surviving message confirmed it.

Then there was what Lizzie said after Andrew’s body was discovered. In speaking to Bridget and Mrs. Churchill, she suggested that Abby was upstairs. She said she thought she had heard her stepmother return and encouraged someone to look for her. This differed from the explanation she had given Andrew and Bridget earlier, when Abby was supposed to be away. Lizzie’s stated reason was that she believed Abby had come back. To investigators, the change was suspicious, especially once Abby was found in the guest room. But Lizzie had asked for an upstairs search; that was not, by itself, a direction to a particular place beside the bed.

The distinction mattered to the account of the morning. If Abby had actually left and returned, someone beyond the household might have been able to confirm the visit. No such person did. If she had never left, Lizzie’s explanation to Andrew had been wrong. Police could establish where Abby’s body was and could compare Lizzie’s statements about her whereabouts. They could not produce the note that would explain why Lizzie had first believed her stepmother was out. What looked like an ordinary errand at the start of her story remained unsupported when officers tried to follow it beyond the house.

A second discrepancy concerned Andrew’s footwear. Lizzie said she had helped her father remove his outdoor shoes before he rested. In the slippers version of the account, he had changed into comfortable footwear for his nap; some accounts also associate Bridget’s recollection with a mention of slippers. Yet Andrew was found wearing his outdoor boots. They are visible in the photographs of his body on the sofa, still on his feet. The scene did not match the action Lizzie had described. Investigators regarded the discrepancy as another reason to question her account of his last minutes.

The boots were a small detail compared with the deaths, but they were an observable one. Unlike an impression of someone’s manner, they could be checked against the body and the photographs. If Lizzie had helped Andrew take them off, the police needed an explanation for why they were on him when he was found. They did not have one. The mismatch did not tell them who had struck him, but it prevented them from accepting all the domestic details of her story as a reliable description of what happened after he entered the sitting room.

Officers were also interested in Lizzie’s clothing. When neighbors and police saw her after the alarm, they reported no visible blood on her face, hands, or hair, and the dress she wore appeared clean. Bridget remembered a blue dress from earlier that morning. The police inspection on the day of the murders was cursory; officers did not seize Lizzie’s wardrobe or examine all her personal effects closely. In an investigation of repeated, close-range blows, they wanted to know whether clothing had been changed or put aside. What had been worn during the morning became important in a way that ordinary household garments had never been before.

On Sunday, August 7, three days after the murders, Alice Russell was staying with the sisters at Second Street. At about eleven that morning, she entered the kitchen and found Lizzie beside the stove with a dress. Alice recognized it as an everyday morning gown Lizzie had worn earlier in the spring: a cotton Bedford cord dress, light blue with small dark figures. Lizzie was tearing it into strips to burn. Asked what she was doing, she said she was getting rid of the old thing because it was covered with paint.

Emma was present and supported that explanation. She said she had advised Lizzie to dispose of the dress because it was no longer fit to wear. To the sisters, as they described it, the garment was an old, spoiled piece of clothing being discarded. Alice saw the act in the circumstances surrounding the house. Police were stationed outside, and the deaths had made ordinary actions subject to scrutiny. She warned her friend: “If I were you I wouldn’t let anybody see me do that, Lizzie.” It was not an accusation that the stains were blood. It was a warning about how the destruction could look.

Lizzie continued. She fed the torn strips into the stove until the garment was consumed. Alice’s account fixed an event that could no longer be checked against the object itself. Bridget had remembered blue clothing on Thursday; Alice had seen a light blue dress destroyed on Sunday. The shared color would invite comparison, but color alone could not make the two garments the same. Emma’s explanation placed the stains before the murders and identified them as paint. Alice could describe what she had watched, not supply the examination that had not occurred before the fabric went into the fire.

Once the dress was burned, nobody could establish whether it was the one Lizzie had worn on the morning of August 4 or an old gown ruined by paint, as the sisters said. It could never be inspected for stains or compared directly with witnesses’ descriptions. What remained was Alice’s recollection of Lizzie at the stove, Emma’s explanation, and the words of the warning. The police still had Lizzie’s account of her own movements to examine. In the coming days, they would ask her to give that account again, this time under oath.

[[ACT:act5]]

For a week after the murders, Lizzie was not under arrest. She remained at Second Street, where Emma had returned from Fairhaven and Alice Russell was staying to help them. Outside, police watched the property while reporters, curious neighbors, and other onlookers gathered nearby. Inside, the sisters continued living among the familiar rooms that had become places of investigation. Lizzie had been questioned and her movements examined, but the authorities had not yet taken her from the house. She was still there when officials came to tell her directly how they regarded her.

On Saturday evening, August 6, Mayor John Coughlin visited with the lead investigator. They informed Lizzie that she was considered a suspect in the deaths of her father and stepmother. No arrest followed that night. The warning did, however, make the direction of the inquiry explicit. She was not simply a bereaved daughter helping the police reconstruct events. Her own conduct was under examination. Three days later, the authorities began a proceeding in which they could require witnesses to answer questions under oath, away from the public gathering outside.

The coroner’s inquest opened on Tuesday, August 9, five days after the deaths, upstairs at the Fall River police station. District Attorney Hosea Knowlton and local officials had consulted in Marshal Hilliard’s office before the hearing began. The doors were closed to reporters and spectators. Witnesses entered individually, with Bridget among the first called. Unlike the trial that would eventually follow, this was an investigative hearing. Its purpose was to gather sworn accounts of what had happened, and Knowlton could press witnesses on differences between those accounts and their earlier statements.

Lizzie was questioned without a lawyer beside her. Her family attorney, Andrew Jackson Jennings, was not permitted to accompany her into the room under the procedure used for the inquest. She faced the district attorney and magistrate alone, without counsel to advise her or object as the questions continued. There was another unusual circumstance. According to the accounts of her treatment, Dr. Bowen had prescribed morphine for her nerves after the murders. She had been taking regular doses during the days before the hearing and gave her evidence while under the medication’s influence.

The questioning continued across three days, from August 9 through August 11. It was the only occasion on which Lizzie would give a sworn account from the witness stand of the events of August 4. Knowlton wanted a sequence he could follow through the house: where she had been when Andrew returned, where she went afterward, and what had occupied her while he was in the sitting room. The questions returned her to short intervals and familiar places. Her answers did not settle into one consistent route.

Asked about the time of her father’s arrival, she first placed herself on the stairs, then in the kitchen. Elsewhere in her account, she was out in the yard eating pears. The shifting locations mattered because they concerned the period when Andrew came through the front entrance and moved into the house. Knowlton was not asking about an incidental outing on another day. He was trying to establish where a person present at the property had been during the morning of two deaths. Lizzie’s answers gave him different versions to set against one another.

The purpose of her barn visit also changed. In her initial account to police, she had described looking for iron or tin to repair a window screen. At the inquest, she said she had been searching for lead sinkers for a planned fishing trip with Emma in Fairhaven. When questioned about the length of the visit, she described spending time eating pears in the barn loft. The explanation now contained both an errand and a period of lingering. What had kept her outside for about 20 minutes was no longer described in the same way as it had been immediately after the deaths.

Knowlton pressed her about going upstairs, about sounds she had heard, and about where she stood while Bridget dealt with the front door. When he confronted her with differences in her statements, she answered, “I don’t know what I have said.” At other moments she challenged the questions themselves: “Some of your questions I have difficulty answering because I don’t know just how you mean them.” The transcript preserved exchanges in which the examiner sought firm distinctions and Lizzie either changed her answer, resisted the wording, or said she could not account for what she had already said.

Officials described her behavior as erratic at times and interpreted the contradictions as evasions. The words alone do not reveal whether they came from deception, grief, medication, or confusion under questioning without counsel. Those conditions complicate the investigators’ reading without resolving the discrepancies. Knowlton and Hilliard nevertheless regarded the sworn account as strengthening their suspicions. They had asked the family member who had been present to explain the morning, and she had left them with incompatible statements about both her location and the errand that supposedly took her away.

On the afternoon of August 11, as the third day of the inquest ended, Marshal Hilliard executed a warrant for Lizzie’s arrest. One week after Andrew and Abby were killed, she was charged with their murders. Charged, not convicted. Brought before the district court, she pleaded not guilty and was transferred to the county jail in Taunton, 20 miles north of Fall River. The move ended the week she had spent at home under observation. She would now wait in custody while the prosecution sought to bring the accusation to trial.

Nearly four months passed before the grand jury made its decision. At the beginning of December, Alice Russell testified about the destruction of the dress. Her evidence gave the grand jury a new account of Lizzie’s conduct after the deaths, beyond what the police had seen during their first searches. On December 2, 1892, Lizzie was indicted for murder. The case went forward to the Superior Court, with a trial scheduled for the following June in New Bedford. Alice’s kitchen encounter had acquired a new consequence: it would be part of the accusation presented in court.

Through the winter and spring, Lizzie remained in Taunton while the lawyers prepared. Knowlton had the inquest transcript, with its changes of location, its different explanations for the barn visit, and the exchanges in which he had challenged her answers. Jennings had been excluded from the room in which those statements were obtained. At trial, he would be able to challenge their use. Before a jury could judge Lizzie’s testimony, the court would have to decide a separate question: whether the jurors would be allowed to hear it at all.

Lizzie Borden’s trial opened in New Bedford on June 5, 1893. Ten months had passed since her arrest. Reporters from Boston and New York crowded the press benches, and dispatches carried the proceedings to readers across the country. Spectators filled the gallery and gathered outside for a glimpse of the defendant. The woman brought into court was known both as a Sunday school teacher and as the daughter accused of killing two members of her household. In New Bedford, those public identities had to give way to a narrower task: deciding the charges on the evidence the court admitted.

Three Superior Court justices presided: Chief Justice Albert Mason and Associate Justices Caleb Blodget and Justin Dewey. The jury consisted of 12 men. Massachusetts barred women from jury service, and Fall River residents were excluded from this panel to reduce local bias. The jurors came from elsewhere in Bristol County and were largely Protestant farmers, tradesmen, and businessmen. The people who would decide the case had not lived alongside the Bordens on Second Street. They would learn about the household through witnesses, exhibits, and the competing accounts offered by the lawyers.

Hosea Knowlton led the prosecution, assisted by William H. Moody, a future United States attorney general. Andrew Jennings was joined for the defense by George D. Robinson, a former governor of Massachusetts. The Borden family’s resources had secured experienced counsel with considerable public standing. Robinson’s skill before a jury would matter, but some of the defense’s most consequential work concerned what the jury would never hear. Before the lawyers could argue the meaning of particular statements, the judges had to rule on whether those statements belonged in the trial.

Lizzie sat beside her counsel in black, with a lace handkerchief and smelling salts. The proceedings did not leave her composed throughout. When the prosecution introduced the victims’ skulls, which had been removed and cleaned during the autopsies to demonstrate the injuries, she fainted in her chair. Attendants revived her before the trial continued. For the jury, the skulls were exhibits in the state’s case. For the woman at the defense table, they were the remains of her father and stepmother, displayed in the courtroom where she faced a capital charge.

The defense challenged the inquest transcript on the grounds that Lizzie had been questioned without legal counsel and while taking prescribed morphine. The judges excluded it. The absence of a lawyer during the inquest became a decisive barrier to using the testimony at trial. As a result, the jurors did not hear the sequence of conflicting sworn answers that had helped persuade the authorities to arrest her. The account that had become so important in the police station was not available to the prosecution as a transcript it could read and argue before this jury.

Lizzie also did not testify at trial. She exercised her right to remain silent, leaving Jennings and Robinson to conduct her defense. The jurors therefore did not hear her questioned directly about the morning, and Knowlton could not ask her from the witness stand to reconcile the conflicting answers given at the inquest. This was not a demonstration that the answers were consistent. It meant the case had to be decided without her sworn explanation of them. Her lawyers could challenge the state’s witnesses without putting her through another examination.

The court excluded the prussic acid testimony as well. Drugstore clerk Eli Bence was the witness through whom Knowlton hoped to introduce the alleged attempt to buy poison on August 3. The prosecution wanted the episode treated as evidence of earlier intent to kill. The defense argued that it had no proper connection to deaths caused by blows rather than poison. The judges sustained the objection. The jury did not hear the pharmacist’s account, so the state could not ask it to infer premeditation from the disputed drugstore visit.

These rulings made the trial materially different from the accumulation of suspicions surrounding the arrest. Readers outside court might know about the inquest and the poison allegation, but the jurors could not decide the case on either. Knowlton still had witnesses who described life inside the household and events around the deaths. His task was to connect those observations to the accusation against Lizzie. The defense’s task was not to supply a complete alternative history of the morning, but to show where the state’s account depended on an inference it had not established.

Bridget’s evidence returned the court to the front door. She stood by her account that she had heard Lizzie laugh from the upstairs landing as Andrew waited to enter. She also supplied an explanation for why someone above her might have laughed. Frustrated by the lock, Bridget said, she had uttered a curse. In her understanding, Lizzie was laughing at that slip of profanity. It was an ordinary explanation for the sound, rather than a claim that the laughter itself revealed knowledge of a crime. But the location remained important: Bridget still placed Lizzie upstairs, while Lizzie’s defense maintained she had been downstairs.

For motive, the prosecution called witnesses who described hostility toward Abby. Acquaintances and a dressmaker testified that Lizzie had spoken of an unhappy home, refused to regard Abby as her mother, and said she hated her. Knowlton used this evidence to challenge the portrait of an entirely harmonious, respectable household. The defense emphasized Lizzie’s churchgoing life and disputed the implication that family resentment had led to murder. These witnesses gave the jury statements to weigh about the relationship, but no one could turn a declaration of dislike into a witnessed account of the attacks.

Alice Russell’s testimony presented another kind of conduct for the jury to interpret. The prosecution relied on her account of the dress as evidence that Lizzie had deliberately destroyed clothing before it could be examined. Alice’s warning to her friend was especially damaging because it showed that the act had seemed unwise even to someone staying in the house to help. Knowlton asked the jurors to understand the incident as concealment. Yet the state did not substantiate its claim that the gown was the one worn during the murders, so its argument depended on what the destruction implied rather than a demonstrated identification.

Emma testified for her sister. She told the jury that disposing of the gown had been her own suggestion and described it as old, faded, and soiled with paint before the deaths. The defense also called a painter, who confirmed that paint had gotten onto a dress during work at the property. This testimony gave the defense support for the explanation Lizzie had offered, rather than leaving it as an unsupported statement from the accused. The jury now had to weigh Alice’s account of suspicious conduct against witnesses who described an ordinary reason for discarding damaged clothing.

Robinson pressed the circumstances of the disposal. If Lizzie had been trying to conceal a bloody gown, he argued, why would she destroy it so openly in the kitchen while police stood outside? The prosecution and defense were drawing opposite conclusions from the same public act. Knowlton treated the burning as purposeful destruction; Robinson treated its openness as inconsistent with calculated concealment. Neither argument changed what Alice had observed. What changed was the explanation the jurors were asked to give it. This was the dress’s role at trial: a disputed inference carried by witnesses, not an exhibit that could answer for itself.

On June 20, before the case went to the jury, Lizzie was given an opportunity to address the court. She said, “I am innocent. I leave it to my counsel to speak for me.” Justice Dewey then instructed the jurors on the standard they had to apply. Suspicion, probability, and conjecture were not enough. The Commonwealth had to establish its accusation beyond a reasonable doubt. The instruction did not ask the men to decide whether some aspects of Lizzie’s conduct seemed troubling. It asked whether the prosecution had proved that she committed the murders.

The decision came quickly. Most accounts put the deliberation at about 90 minutes; others describe a shorter interval. There is no need to assign an exact minute to every stage of that afternoon to understand the result. After two weeks of evidence and argument, the jury did not spend days weighing the case. The men returned to the courtroom on June 20, and the foreman announced their unanimous verdict: “Not guilty.” Lizzie bent forward, covered her face with her hands, and sobbed. Cheers and applause broke out in the gallery and carried to the crowd outside.

The state had presented opportunity, alleged motive, and conduct it considered suspicious. It had not supplied a direct witness to the killings or a conclusive connection between Lizzie and the acts themselves. The jurors were left with inferences that did not remove reasonable doubt. A verdict of not guilty was not proof of innocence, and it was not proof that anyone else had committed the crimes. It meant the prosecution had not proved its charges under the law.

For Lizzie, the consequence was final. The protection against double jeopardy meant Massachusetts could not try her again for Andrew and Abby’s murders. Chief Justice Mason discharged her, ending the confinement that had begun the previous August. Emma, Jennings, and Robinson were there as she left the defense table. Outside the courthouse, the interest that had filled the gallery and the newspapers would continue. But she no longer had to return to the jail in Taunton. Lizzie Borden walked out of the New Bedford courthouse free.

The public story had already traveled far beyond the evidence. On October 10, 1892, while Lizzie was still awaiting trial, the Boston Globe published a front-page article by Henry Trickey claiming that she had been pregnant and that Andrew had threatened to evict her. The story was fabricated. Two days later, the newspaper printed a front-page retraction and apologized for the false accusation. It was not a disputed witness recollection or an interpretation of something found at Second Street. A major newspaper had supplied an invented explanation for the crimes and presented it to readers as news.

That distinction became harder to maintain as the case entered folklore. The familiar rhyme says Lizzie took an axe, gave her mother forty whacks, then gave her father forty-one. Its rhythm made it easier to remember than the record. The killings involved a hatchet, Abby was Lizzie’s stepmother, and the medical counts were far below the rhyme’s totals. Most consequentially, the verse named the killer without qualification. Repetition turned an accusation into something that sounded as settled as a childhood fact. Over the following century, films, plays, and television dramatizations would repeatedly reshape the woman and the household around competing versions of the story.

Rumors also followed Bridget Sullivan, who left Fall River after the trial and eventually settled in Butte, Montana. Later accounts alleged that, before her death there in 1948, she had told her sister she had lied or changed parts of her testimony to help Lizzie. No written confession, sworn account, or contemporaneous record supports that story. It emerged as secondhand rumor, not a documented correction by Bridget to the evidence she gave in court. It cannot settle the disagreement about the stairs or disclose what she knew beyond her testimony.

Nor did the authorities bring a different person to trial. Lizzie remained the principal focus of public suspicion after her acquittal, but no one else was charged. The continued attention to her sometimes obscures how many proposed solutions accumulated around other people. Some began with a genuine connection to the household; others depended on relationships or events that could not be established. A plausible place in a theory was not the same thing as evidence placing someone at either attack. The distinction matters when a name recurs often enough to become familiar.

John Morse was an early example. He had arrived unexpectedly the day before the murders, had discussed property with Andrew, and had slept in the very room where Abby died. Those circumstances made him an obvious person for the police to question. But his movements after leaving that morning were corroborated. He gave detailed information about streetcars, transit workers, and people he had met, and outside witnesses supported his account of visiting relatives across town. The police dismissed him as a suspect. His overnight presence explained why the guest room needed attention; it did not overcome the evidence of where he went afterward.

An outsider remained another possibility in later theories: an unknown intruder, a resentful tenant, or someone with a grudge against Andrew’s business practices. Such proposals drew on Andrew’s dealings and on Lizzie’s warning to Alice that he might have enemies. But no identified outsider was connected to the killings. The already established problems of access and the interval between the deaths still had to be answered, and no witness supplied the missing person. A general possibility that a wealthy businessman had enemies did not identify one who entered this house that morning.

Other theories named Emma, Bridget, or a supposed illegitimate son called William Borden, or proposed paternal abuse as the cause of the violence. Records discredited the claim that William was Andrew’s son. The remaining allegations likewise never acquired contemporary documentation, physical evidence, or verifiable testimony establishing them as explanations of the murders. Grouped together, they show the range of stories people have offered to fill the gaps, not a succession of discoveries. None became a proved case against another person.

For Lizzie and Emma, the deaths also brought a change in material life. They inherited from an estate valued at about $300,000 and left Second Street for the neighborhood their father had declined to join. On The Hill, they bought a spacious, modern Victorian house with 14 rooms and named it Maplecroft. The sisters now had the home and resources that Andrew’s frugality had withheld from their daily lives. They could move out of the rooms where they had grown up and where the killings had occurred, though leaving the address did not remove the public attention attached to their name.

Fall River did not broadly welcome Lizzie back into its social life. Old acquaintances avoided her, and the town shunned her. She lived largely in seclusion at Maplecroft, with private carriage rides and occasional trips away. Money provided comfort and distance, but not the familiar standing she had once enjoyed through church and community work. The crowds had cheered at the courthouse, yet the reception at home was different. The accusation continued to shape how neighbors and former friends treated her long after the trial ended.

Emma remained with her until 1905. Then a dispute neither sister publicly explained ended their shared household. Emma moved out of Maplecroft and left Fall River permanently. They never saw or spoke to each other again. The sister who had looked after Lizzie since childhood, returned from Fairhaven after the deaths, and testified in her defense was no longer part of her daily life. There is no documented explanation from either woman that resolves their separation, and it should not be made into a retrospective confession of anything about August 4.

Lizzie died of pneumonia on June 1, 1927, at 66. Emma died nine days later. Both were buried in the Borden family plot at Oak Grove Cemetery in Fall River, near Andrew and Abby. The house on Second Street survived them and now operates as a museum and historic inn. Visitors can enter the sitting room, climb the front stairs, and see the guest bedroom. The place is tangible in a way many of the stories are not: the rooms remain, even where the record of what happened inside them stops.

Abby is less often remembered as a person than as one of the bodies in those rooms. She had spent nearly three decades in the household, tending its ordinary needs amid relationships other people described in sharply different ways. The arguments about money, the trial, and the rhyme gave her a narrow place in the story of her own death. Before any of that, on the morning of August 4, she had a guest room to put in order.

At around nine, Abby went up the front stairs to change the bed John Morse had slept in. She carried clean linens into the room and set about the familiar work of making it ready again: smoothing the coverlet, adjusting the pillow shams, and dusting the furniture. Downstairs, Bridget had windows to wash. Andrew had gone to conduct his business. Abby was upstairs, attending to a room another member of the family had just used. It was the last ordinary task for which the accounts place her alive.

She never walked back down. Someone reached the guest room while she was there. Who climbed those stairs?
