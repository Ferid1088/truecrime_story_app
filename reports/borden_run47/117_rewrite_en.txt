[[ACT:act1]]

At about half past ten on the morning of August 4, 1892, Andrew Borden came home to Second Street in Fall River, Massachusetts, and tried his key in the front door. He couldn’t get in. Inside, the family’s maid, Bridget Sullivan, came to open it for him. As she struggled with the lock, she heard someone laugh. The sound, she said, came from the top of the front staircase, near the guest bedroom. She recognized the voice as Andrew’s younger daughter, Lizzie.

Lizzie denied it. She said she hadn’t laughed and hadn’t been standing upstairs. When her father returned, she insisted, she was on the ground floor.

This was not a disagreement about the exact time someone crossed a room. Bridget placed Lizzie at the head of the stairs. Lizzie placed herself downstairs. Before the morning was over, the room beside that upstairs landing would become one of two places in the house where someone had been killed.

A little after eleven, Bridget was in her third-floor room, resting after washing windows, when Lizzie called to her: “Come down quickly. Father is dead. Someone came in and killed him.”

Bridget hurried downstairs. Lizzie was near the back entrance. Next door, Adelaide Churchill noticed her standing at the doorway and called across to ask what was wrong. “Oh, Mrs. Churchill, do come over,” Lizzie answered. “Someone has killed father.”

Mrs. Churchill went to the house. In the downstairs sitting room, 70-year-old Andrew Borden lay stretched out on the sofa. Someone had struck him repeatedly in the head and face with a hatchet. His skull was fractured, his features almost unrecognizable. Blood covered the cushions and had spattered the wall behind him. He had returned from his morning business downtown and, within a short time, had been killed in his own sitting room.

There was another member of the family to find: Andrew’s wife, Abby. Bridget and Mrs. Churchill went to the front staircase and climbed toward the second floor. Beside the landing was the guest bedroom. Inside, between the bed and the dressing bureau, Abby Borden lay face down on the carpet. She, too, had been struck repeatedly in the head. Blood had pooled beneath her body. She was 64.

The women had gone upstairs looking for someone who might help explain what had happened below. Instead, they had found a second victim. The staircase Bridget had used to let Andrew into the house now led directly to another murder scene.

Outside was a busy manufacturing city. Fall River’s textile mills and commercial streets surrounded a household that kept itself closely shut off from the neighborhood. The wooden house at 92 Second Street stood only a block from the commercial center, but its exterior doors were routinely locked. It was not a remote place where an attack could pass unnoticed for lack of people nearby. Neighbors were close enough to see Lizzie at the back doorway and come when she called.

At the time of the killings, the only people known to be in the house besides the victims were Lizzie and Bridget. Lizzie was 32, unmarried, and living with her father and stepmother. Bridget was 26 and had worked for the family for three years. Each said she had seen nothing of either attack.

There was no sign that someone had forced a door or window. The house had not been ransacked, and valuables had not disappeared. That left the first officers with a problem more specific than the discovery of two bodies. Whoever had killed Andrew and Abby had moved through an occupied home in daylight without either surviving woman reporting a sight of the attacker.

Shortly after a quarter past eleven, a telephone call from a neighboring stable reached the Fall River police. Marshal Rufus Hilliard received word that Andrew Borden had been killed. The department was shorthanded. August 4 was the day of its annual picnic at Rocky Point, and many officers were away.

Officer George Allen was sent to Second Street. He arrived around twenty past eleven, as neighbors began gathering near the property. More officers followed. They entered a house with two separate murder scenes, people arriving to help, and no obvious route by which an attacker had broken in. Their first task was to establish who had been there and where each person had been.

The locked doors did not prove that no outsider could have entered. But an outsider would have needed to get in, move between the rooms, and leave without being seen. No witness reported seeing an unfamiliar person enter or escape. Nor was there an obvious robbery to explain why anyone had come.

For the people already inside, the questions were different. How could two attacks have taken place without their knowledge? What had they heard? What had they been doing? And how accurately could anyone reconstruct movements that had seemed ordinary until the bodies were found?

One of those questions was already contained in the accounts of Andrew’s return. Bridget said Lizzie had been above her, at the top of the front stairs. Lizzie said she had been downstairs. The distance between those two places was small enough to cross in moments. The difference between the statements would last through the investigation and into court.

The case became a national sensation, and Lizzie Borden became a name known far beyond Fall River. But the legal outcome can be stated plainly: no one was ever convicted, and the murders have never been officially solved.

[[ACT:act2]]

Lizzie Andrew Borden was born in Fall River on July 19, 1860. In the summer of 1892, she had just turned 32. Much of her life was organized around the Central Congregational Church. She taught Sunday school, took part in religious societies, and volunteered through church committees and local hospital work. To people who knew her through those activities, she was an unmarried woman from an established family, engaged in the respectable work expected of someone in her position.

Her older sister, Emma, was 41. Their mother, Sarah, had died when Lizzie was a child, and Emma had often looked after her younger sister. Both women remained at home, unmarried and financially dependent on their father, though property transactions had given them money of their own. Emma was described as quiet and reserved. When the murders occurred, she was visiting friends in Fairhaven, about 15 miles away.

Their father, Andrew Jackson Borden, had made a substantial fortune without adopting the way of life that usually accompanied one. He began as a furniture maker, producing furniture and caskets, and expanded into real estate, textile investments, and banking. By 1892, he was president of the Union Savings Bank. His estate was estimated at about $300,000.

Other wealthy Fall River families lived in the fashionable district called The Hill. Andrew kept his family on Second Street, near the mills and downtown businesses, in a modest house without modern conveniences such as indoor plumbing. He was known for his frugality. The contrast between what he could afford and how he chose to live was part of the family’s daily circumstances, not merely a detail noticed after his death.

Andrew had married his second wife, Abby Durfee Gray, in June 1865, roughly two years after Sarah’s death. Abby had been in the household for nearly three decades. For most of Lizzie’s life, she had been the woman managing the home. But the length of that relationship did not settle how Lizzie and Emma felt about her.

Money was one source of tension. Reports published after the murders described a dispute around 1887, when Andrew bought a house for Abby’s half-sister to occupy. According to those accounts, his daughters objected to family money benefiting Abby’s relatives. Lizzie reportedly argued that whatever Andrew did for his wife’s people, he should do for his own children. The story became an important part of the proposed motive for murder, though the accounts of the feud were not equally well supported.

The property dealings themselves were more concrete. Andrew transferred houses to his daughters for the nominal price of $1 each. Several years later, the sisters sold the properties back to him for $5,000. They had gained independent funds, but the arrangement did not lead them to leave the household. All three women continued living under Andrew’s roof, with the eventual division of his much larger estate still ahead.

Accounts of their relations differed sharply. Some acquaintances described coldness and separate meals, with the daughters avoiding the table shared by Andrew and Abby. Witnesses later said Lizzie believed Abby had married for money. Other accounts, including Lizzie’s formal testimony, described everyday relations as cordial. Those differences matter: a household could be divided without every encounter being an argument, and later testimony about hostility did not give a complete picture of years spent together.

One statement from the morning of the murders is documented. When an officer referred to Abby as Lizzie’s mother, Lizzie corrected him: “She is not my mother, sir. She is my father’s wife. My mother died when I was a child.” Lizzie and Emma called her Mrs. Borden. The correction established the relationship precisely, while also preserving a distinction the daughters had maintained for years.

The house made separation unusually easy. It had originally been built as a multi-family dwelling. Upstairs, there was no hallway allowing someone to walk freely from the front rooms to the rear. Rooms connected directly through doors, some of which were kept fastened. A door stood between Lizzie’s bedroom and Andrew and Abby’s bedroom, but it was bolted on both sides.

To reach her father’s room without opening that barrier, Lizzie had to go down the front stairs, cross the ground floor through the sitting room and dining room, and climb the back stairs from the kitchen. The front stairs served Lizzie’s room and the guest bedroom. The rear stairs led toward Andrew and Abby’s room and the domestic quarters above. Two people upstairs could be physically close yet separated by a route that took them through much of the house.

Bridget Sullivan slept on the third floor. An Irish domestic servant, she had lived and worked with the Bordens for three years. With Emma away, the regular household consisted of Bridget, Lizzie, Andrew, and Abby. On August 3, an unexpected visitor joined them.

John Vinnicum Morse was Sarah Borden’s brother, making him Lizzie and Emma’s maternal uncle. He had spent much of his adult life farming in Iowa and did not often visit. He arrived unannounced that Wednesday afternoon while in the East on personal business. Andrew invited him to stay for several days so they could discuss property and investments. Morse was given the guest bedroom at the top of the front stairs.

The household had already been disturbed by illness. Andrew, Abby, and Bridget had suffered severe stomach trouble in the days before the murders. Mutton left out in the summer heat appears to have caused food poisoning, but some in the house feared deliberate poisoning. Andrew suspected someone might have tried to harm the family. Food that had been kept too long and fears of an outside enemy became entangled before the police had any reason to investigate either.

That evening, between seven and nine, Lizzie visited her friend Alice Russell. Alice later described her as anxious and depressed. Lizzie spoke of a threat she could not identify, saying she feared something terrible would happen. She mentioned her father’s business affairs and the possibility that he had enemies. She did not name an attacker or describe a specific plan. Alice heard the fears of a daughter in an uneasy, ailing household.

Another account concerned something said to have happened earlier on August 3. At a local pharmacy, a woman matching Lizzie’s description reportedly asked for prussic acid, or hydrogen cyanide. When refused without a prescription, she said she wanted it to clean a sealskin cape. The clerk again refused. Pharmacy staff later supported the account.

Lizzie denied going to the shop or trying to buy poison. Her attorneys maintained that another woman had been mistaken for her. The pharmacy witnesses stood by their identification, but accounts of the encounter remained disputed, and no sale had taken place. The reported request left an accusation of a very different kind beside the family’s stomach illness and Lizzie’s fears: that, on the day before the murders, someone believed to be Lizzie had tried to obtain a deadly poison.

The household began moving early on Thursday, August 4. Accounts differ about who ate breakfast together. Some place Andrew, Abby, John Morse, and Bridget at the table around seven, with Lizzie still upstairs. Others have Lizzie joining her parents, or coming down later to eat alone. The departures are clearer. Around a quarter to nine, Morse left to visit relatives across town, intending to return by noon. Andrew went out around nine to conduct business at the banks and in the commercial district.

Abby went upstairs to straighten the guest bedroom and make the bed Morse had used. This was the last time any witness confirmed seeing her alive. Downstairs, Lizzie told Bridget about a sale on dress goods at a local department store and suggested she might want to go. Bridget, still unwell after the household’s stomach illness, declined. She stayed to wash the ground-floor windows.

Andrew returned around half past ten. The front door was fastened, and Bridget let him in. Within the next part of the morning, he lay down on the sitting-room sofa. A little after eleven, Lizzie raised the alarm. By the time the police and physicians began examining the house, both bodies had been found: Andrew downstairs, Abby in the guest room.

The attacks had been concentrated on the victims’ heads. Andrew’s skull and facial bones were fractured. Abby had sustained repeated wounds to the back of her head and neck. Neither showed signs of having been able to raise a hand in defense. The injuries indicated a sharp, heavy instrument used at close range.

The reported counts differ. The BBC gives eleven blows to Andrew; Legends of America gives ten, the number of head wounds also recorded by medical examiner Dr. William Dolan. Abby’s injuries are generally counted as 18 or 19, with Dolan recording 18 head wounds and one to her back. The small differences between those tallies do not alter the nature of the attacks, but they would later be overwhelmed by a much larger number in the popular rhyme.

More important to the investigation was the condition of the bodies. Upstairs, Abby was cold to the touch. The blood beneath her was dark, coagulated, and dry. Downstairs, Andrew’s body was still warm, and liquid blood remained at his wounds. These were not two people killed together and then discovered in different rooms. Abby had died first.

That sequence changed the morning the police were trying to reconstruct. While Andrew was still out conducting business, his wife had been attacked upstairs. After he came home, the guest room remained undisturbed long enough for him to settle on the sofa. Whatever had happened to Abby had not led anyone to raise an alarm before Andrew’s death.

The physicians’ estimates of the interval were less exact than the order itself. The coroner placed Abby’s death between nine and half past ten; several assessments put it around half past nine. Andrew’s death was estimated between half past ten and ten past eleven. Individual medical opinions suggested an interval of an hour to an hour and a half, with some allowing nearly two hours. Those were estimates from observations and accounts, not times established by a clock.

Still, there was no scientific doubt about the essential conclusion: the victims had been killed some time apart. The long gap meant the police were investigating more than one sudden eruption of violence. A person responsible for both attacks had remained undetected in or around the property between them, while ordinary movements continued and Andrew left and returned.

Investigators searched the cellar for an implement capable of causing the wounds. They found several edged tools, including two axes and two intact hatchets. Another item, in a box of old tools, drew particular attention: a hatchet head with its handle missing. A short piece of wood remained in the opening where the handle had fitted.

Several officers thought the break looked fresh. The prosecution would argue that the blade, roughly three and a half inches across, matched the wounds in the victims’ skulls. Under that theory, the killer had snapped off the wooden handle because it could have absorbed blood, then put ash or cellar dust on the head to make the tool look as though it had been there undisturbed.

But the officers’ observations did not agree. One contradicted the claim that the handle had recently been broken, saying the wood did not show a fresh snap. That disagreement concerned the feature that made the tool seem suspicious in the first place. If the damage was old, a broken hatchet in a box of disused tools had a different meaning.

The coating on the metal was disputed as well. Defense testimony described a large accumulation of ash in the cellar that coated the surrounding objects. A thick layer on the hatchet could therefore be ordinary cellar dirt rather than something deliberately applied to disguise a recently used weapon. The same surface that the prosecution interpreted as concealment could be explained by where the tool had been stored.

Chemical examination did not establish conclusive traces of blood on the blade. The proposed connection rested on the dimensions of the head, the apparent condition of the wood, and the interpretation of the ash. Each had to support the next. The blade’s possible fit did not settle whether this particular hatchet had been used, and the officers could not even agree on whether its handle had just been broken.

Elsewhere in the cellar, in the washroom, police found a pail containing blood-soaked pieces of cloth. Lizzie explained that she was menstruating and that the cloths were connected with her period. Her physician, Dr. Seabury Bowen, confirmed that she was menstruating. The discovery therefore came with a domestic explanation from Lizzie and support for that explanation from the family doctor.

Accounts of what happened to the cloths are less secure than the fact that investigators encountered them. According to investigative accounts and later reviews, the officers accepted the explanation and did not seize the rags for chemical examination. Some accounts describe the pail as noted and left where it was; others emphasize how little attention it received. The surviving descriptions vary in detail, and they do not supply a completed scientific examination of the blood.

The circumstances also reflected the investigation’s period. Police methods for handling biological evidence were limited, and menstrual matters were treated with pronounced social discomfort. Neither circumstance determines what was in the pail. What it helps explain is why an object that attracted attention could be set aside after an explanation, rather than preserved for a more thorough inquiry.

Forensic science in 1892 could not provide the comparisons that blood typing or later methods might have allowed. Investigators could observe stains and conduct the chemical tests available to them, but they could not reliably pursue microscopic traces or identify whose blood a minute sample contained. In this case, some potentially important material was disputed, some was not retained, and some produced no conclusive result.

Searches also failed to uncover hidden, blood-drenched clothing. No missing money or jewelry supplied a robbery motive. No forced entry explained access to the house. Those absences narrowed what the police could describe, but they did not identify the person who had struck the blows.

The order of the deaths was the strongest consequence of the physical examination. Abby’s body had been upstairs for a substantial time before Andrew was killed below. More than an hour appears to have separated the attacks. During the morning, the people known to have been on the premises were Lizzie, Bridget, and, before his departure, John Morse, along with the victims. The bodies established a sequence that none of the surviving witnesses had reported seeing.

The distinction was important. The physicians could say which victim died first and could estimate the interval. Police could identify an edged tool that might have made the wounds. They could describe the rooms, the doors, and what had or had not been found in a search. But the physical evidence could not place the weapon in a particular person’s hands or establish whether the killer belonged to the household or had come from outside.

The investigation now depended on the movements and statements of the people who had been there. In particular, the officers needed to understand what Lizzie said had happened during the interval after her father returned, and why she believed her stepmother was not in the house.

Lizzie’s account to the police began with an explanation for Abby’s absence. Earlier that morning, she said, a messenger had delivered a note asking Abby to visit a sick friend. Abby had gone to answer it. Bridget was washing windows, Andrew was downtown, and nothing had happened to alert Lizzie to trouble.

When Andrew came home, Lizzie told him about the message and the visit. He settled on the sitting-room sofa. She then went out to the barn, she told officers, to look for a piece of iron to repair a window screen. She estimated that she had been away for about 20 minutes. Returning to the house shortly after eleven, she found her father dead.

In that account, her absence from the sitting room coincided with the time in which an intruder must have attacked him. Lizzie had not seen anyone enter or leave. She offered the barn trip as an explanation of where she had been, not an account of witnessing the crime. The police had to examine both the errand and the assumptions on which the rest of her story depended.

Some officers were troubled by how she answered. Their notes and later reports described her as unusually calm and controlled. They saw no tears or obvious panic. To them, her composure seemed inconsistent with what she had just discovered. Others interpreted the same behavior as shock or an effort to preserve her dignity. Those impressions helped direct suspicion toward her, even though observers did not agree about what the behavior meant.

The note was something officers could try to verify. Marshal Rufus Hilliard and his men searched the house for it, looking through places where a message might have been put or discarded. They did not find one. Lizzie suggested Abby might have burned it. Inquiries outside the house produced no sick friend who said she had sent for Abby, and no messenger was identified.

The message remained important because Lizzie had used it to explain why Abby was away. She had mentioned it to Bridget and to Andrew. If Abby had left on such an errand, her failure to appear downstairs would have seemed ordinary. But the search yielded no independent confirmation that the errand had existed. Police were left with Lizzie’s statement about a document nobody could produce and a sender nobody could find.

After Andrew’s body was discovered, Lizzie expressed a different understanding of where Abby might be. She told Mrs. Churchill and Bridget that she believed she had heard her stepmother come back and suggested they look upstairs. That was how the search moved toward the guest room.

Investigators regarded the change with suspicion, but the statements were not necessarily descriptions of the same moment. Lizzie’s explanation allowed for Abby to have gone out and then returned. Saying she thought Abby was upstairs did not, by itself, show that she knew where the body lay. The difficulty was that the departure on which that explanation rested could not be confirmed, while Abby had in fact remained upstairs long enough for her blood to dry.

Another part of Lizzie’s account was more directly at odds with what the officers could see. Describing Andrew’s preparations to rest, she said she had helped him remove his shoes. In versions of the story, he then put on slippers before lying down. Some accounts also associate a mention of slippers with Bridget’s recollection.

Andrew was found wearing his outdoor boots. They remained laced on his feet as he lay on the sofa, and the photographs of the sitting room recorded them. Whatever had been said about helping him change his footwear, he had not died in slippers.

To the police, that was a troubling detail to get wrong. It concerned an action Lizzie said she had participated in, not a sound she might have misheard from another room. They suspected she had added an ordinary, attentive exchange with her father to make his final minutes appear settled and uneventful. That was their interpretation; the boots themselves established the discrepancy, not the reason she had described it differently.

The questions about her clothing involved a different kind of difficulty. When neighbors and police arrived, they saw no visible blood on Lizzie’s face, hands, or hair, and her dress appeared clean. Bridget remembered her coming downstairs that morning in a blue dress. But the first inspection of the house was cursory, and officers did not seize her wardrobe or make a detailed examination of her personal effects.

The attacks had left blood on furniture, walls, and carpet. Police naturally wanted to know what the person responsible might have been wearing and whether anything had been changed or concealed. Bridget’s recollection supplied a color, but it did not provide a garment that investigators could immediately set apart and examine as the clothing Lizzie had worn throughout the morning.

Three days later, Alice Russell saw something that would make the clothing question central to the case.

Alice was staying at Second Street with the sisters after the murders. On Sunday, August 7, at about eleven in the morning, she went into the kitchen and found Lizzie by the stove with a dress. It was light blue cotton Bedford cord patterned with small dark figures. Alice recognized it as an everyday morning dress Lizzie had worn earlier that spring.

Lizzie was tearing the fabric into strips to put into the fire. Alice asked what she was doing. Lizzie said she intended to burn the old dress because it was covered with paint.

Emma was there. She confirmed that she had advised Lizzie to get rid of it because it was no longer fit to wear. The explanation was not that a dress had suddenly become unnecessary after the murders, but that an old garment had already been spoiled. Alice nevertheless saw the danger in what her friend was doing. Officers were stationed outside the house, and suspicion was already gathering around Lizzie.

“If I were you I wouldn’t let anybody see me do that, Lizzie,” she said.

Lizzie did not argue with her, but she continued. The strips went into the kitchen stove, and the dress was consumed. The act was done in a shared room, with Emma and Alice present, rather than during a moment when Lizzie was alone. That openness would later become part of the dispute over what it meant.

For Alice, the event was memorable enough to preserve in detail: the garment, the stove, Lizzie’s explanation, Emma’s agreement, and her own warning. Her knowledge of the dress came from having seen it before and recognizing it in the kitchen. Bridget’s memory of the murder morning was different in kind: she remembered Lizzie wearing blue. The two observations concerned clothing of a similar color, but they were not one continuous observation of the same garment.

That distinction could no longer be checked against the dress itself. Investigators would have to rely on what the people in the kitchen remembered, what others knew about the paint, and what anyone could say about Lizzie’s clothing on Thursday. The event had occurred after the police had begun watching the property, but before they had taken possession of the garment.

Nobody could establish whether the dress burned on Sunday was the one Lizzie had worn on the morning of August 4. It might have been an old paint-stained dress, as she and Emma said; it might have been something investigators had reason to want preserved. Its fabric and stains could never be examined. What remained was Alice Russell’s account of Lizzie feeding the torn pieces into the stove.

For a week after the murders, Lizzie remained at home. Emma returned from Fairhaven, and Alice Russell stayed with the sisters. Outside, reporters, neighbors, and curious townspeople gathered along Second Street. Police watched the property. Inside, the women continued living in the rooms that had become the subject of questions across the country.

The authorities had not yet arrested Lizzie, but they made their suspicions known. On the evening of Saturday, August 6, Mayor John Coughlin and the lead investigator went to the house and informed her that she was considered a suspect. They left without taking her into custody. She remained there through the following days, including the Sunday morning when Alice saw the dress destroyed.

On Tuesday, August 9, five days after the killings, officials opened a coroner’s inquest on the upper floor of the Fall River police station. District Attorney Hosea Knowlton had consulted with local officials in Marshal Hilliard’s office. The inquiry then proceeded behind closed doors, with the public and press excluded. Witnesses were called individually. Bridget was among the first.

An inquest was an investigation, not a trial, but its witnesses answered under oath. Lizzie entered without an attorney beside her. Under the procedure then in use, her family lawyer, Andrew Jackson Jennings, was not permitted to accompany her into the room. She would have to hear the questions and formulate her answers without his advice.

There was also a medical circumstance. Dr. Bowen had prescribed morphine for her nerves after the murders. According to the accounts of the proceedings, she was still receiving the medication when she testified. Across sessions from August 9 through August 11, she faced repeated questioning about the morning. This would be the only occasion on which she gave a sworn account from the witness stand of what had happened.

Knowlton wanted a sequence he could follow through the house. Where had Lizzie been when Andrew returned? Where had she gone afterward? What had she heard? Her answers did not remain stable. At first she placed herself on the stairs. Then she said she had been downstairs in the kitchen. At another point, she described being in the yard eating pears.

These were not merely different descriptions of one place. They put her in separate parts of the property at a moment already disputed by Bridget’s account. The questioning returned to the front door and to what Lizzie had been doing while Bridget worked the lock. Instead of fixing her location more firmly, her answers gave the officials several versions to compare.

The barn errand changed as well. In her first account to police, Lizzie had said she went to find iron or tin for a window screen. At the inquest, she described looking for lead sinkers for a fishing trip she planned to take with Emma in Fairhaven. Asked how she had spent about 20 minutes there, she spoke of lingering to eat pears.

A trip to fetch a piece for a repair and a search for fishing weights could both be ordinary errands. But they were different explanations for the same absence. That absence was particularly important because Lizzie placed herself outside the house when Andrew was attacked. Knowlton was not asking about a minor outing on an otherwise uneventful day. He was testing the period in which her account put her away from the sitting room.

The questions continued across the three days. Her descriptions of going upstairs, hearing sounds, and moving between rooms did not yield a single consistent route. When confronted with differences between what she had said and what she was now saying, she answered, “I don’t know what I have said.”

Sources describe her manner as erratic at times. Some replies seemed vague or evasive to the questioners; others were resistant. Lizzie herself objected to the difficulty of understanding the examination: “Some of your questions I have difficulty answering because I don’t know just how you mean them.”

To the investigators, the changes looked like an account coming apart under pressure. The transcript preserves those changes, but it cannot establish what produced them. Deliberate evasion, grief, the effects of prescribed morphine, and the strain of answering without counsel offered competing ways of reading her performance. None can be selected simply from the fact that she contradicted herself.

What the authorities did have was a sworn record that failed to reconcile her movements. They had brought the surviving daughter in to explain the morning and had obtained answers that raised further questions. The inquiry had not produced an admission. It had strengthened their judgment that she should be charged.

On the afternoon of Thursday, August 11, as the third day’s proceedings ended, Marshal Hilliard executed a warrant for Lizzie Borden’s arrest. One week after the killings, she was taken into custody and charged with the murders of her father and stepmother.

She was charged, not convicted. Brought before the district court, she pleaded not guilty. She was then transferred to the county jail in Taunton, 20 miles north of Fall River, to await further proceedings. The investigation that had kept her under observation at home now held her in a cell.

Nearly four months passed before the grand jury’s decision. During that time, the case remained one built from circumstances and statements. An arrest had identified the person the authorities intended to prosecute; an indictment would determine whether the accusation went forward to trial.

In early December, Alice Russell appeared before the grand jury and told what she had seen at the kitchen stove. Her account brought the destruction of the dress into the formal case. It gave prosecutors an event after the murders that they could present as possible concealment, rather than another disputed recollection of the morning itself.

On December 2, 1892, the grand jury indicted Lizzie Borden for murder. The case was sent to the Superior Court, with a trial set for the following June in New Bedford. Lizzie remained in Taunton as the lawyers prepared.

Knowlton now had to turn an investigation into evidence admissible before a jury. The distinction would matter most where the authorities had obtained Lizzie’s own words. Her inquest testimony contained the contradictions that had helped bring about her arrest, but it had been taken in a closed inquiry under procedures the defense intended to challenge.

A jury could not simply be handed everything the investigators had heard and asked to decide which story sounded most suspicious. The court first had to decide what evidence the law allowed it to consider. The most consequential question approaching trial was whether those twelve jurors would ever hear Lizzie’s inquest testimony.

Lizzie Borden’s trial opened in New Bedford on June 5, 1893. She had spent ten months in custody. Reporters from Boston and New York filled the press benches, and telegraph dispatches carried the proceedings to readers far from Massachusetts. Spectators crowded the gallery and gathered outside to see the woman whose name had become attached to the murders.

Three Superior Court justices presided: Chief Justice Albert Mason, Caleb Blodget, and Justin Dewey. The jury consisted of twelve men. Massachusetts law did not permit women to serve. The jurors came from Bristol County, with Fall River residents excluded to reduce local bias, and included farmers, tradesmen, and businessmen.

Hosea Knowlton led the prosecution, assisted by William H. Moody, who would later become United States attorney general. Andrew Jennings represented Lizzie alongside George D. Robinson, a former governor of Massachusetts. The family’s wealth had enabled her to retain a defense with both intimate knowledge of the case and substantial courtroom authority.

Lizzie sat beside her lawyers in black, with a lace handkerchief and smelling salts. The prosecution’s presentation included the victims’ skulls, removed and cleaned during the autopsies to demonstrate the wounds. When confronted with her father’s fractured skull, she fainted in her chair and had to be revived before the proceedings continued.

The jurors were there to decide whether the Commonwealth had proved that Lizzie committed the murders. But before they could evaluate the accusation, the justices had to settle which parts of the state’s case they were permitted to hear.

The defense challenged the inquest transcript. Jennings and Robinson argued that Lizzie’s statements had been improperly obtained during questioning without counsel, also pointing to her prescribed morphine. The court excluded the record because she had been questioned without a lawyer. The contradictory sworn account that had helped lead investigators to arrest her would not be laid before the jury.

Lizzie did not testify at trial. She was entitled to remain silent, and the prosecution could not require her to take the stand. The jurors therefore did not hear her questioned about the stairs, kitchen, pears, or the changing purpose of her barn errand. Her lawyers conducted the defense without putting her through a second examination.

The court also excluded the pharmacy evidence. Knowlton wanted clerk Eli Bence to describe the alleged attempt to buy prussic acid on August 3. He argued that it suggested preparation for murder. The defense answered that neither victim had died of poison and that the disputed encounter did not belong in a trial for these attacks. The justices sustained the objection. The jury did not hear the pharmacist’s account, and the prosecution could not use it to establish prior intent.

Those rulings did not settle whether Lizzie had told the truth at the inquest or whether the pharmacy witnesses had identified the right woman. They determined what could be used in this prosecution. The case heard in court was therefore different from the accumulation of allegations already circulating outside it.

Bridget did testify. She returned the jurors to the moment Andrew was waiting at the front door and supplied her explanation for the laughter she said she had heard. While struggling with the lock, she had muttered a curse. She believed Lizzie had laughed at that slip of profanity from the landing above.

That explanation gave the sound an ordinary cause. Bridget was not describing laughter at something she knew had happened in the guest room. She was describing what she understood as a response to her own frustration at the door. Yet her account still placed Lizzie upstairs, and Lizzie still denied having been there or having laughed. The context changed how the moment might be understood without removing the disagreement over location.

To establish motive, Knowlton called witnesses who described Lizzie’s resentment toward Abby. Acquaintances and a dressmaker spoke of an unhappy household and said Lizzie had expressed hatred for her stepmother. Her refusal to call Abby her mother formed part of that picture. The prosecution sought to show that the divisions over family and property had been serious, not the harmless differences the defense preferred to emphasize.

The defense could point to a life organized around church and charitable work. The prosecution answered with testimony about what Lizzie had said in less public settings. Neither portrait was a direct account of the killings. The argument was over whether the woman known in the community and the daughter described inside the household were being represented fairly, and what either portrait could establish about the crime.

Alice Russell’s testimony gave the state something more concrete than an account of hostility. She described what she had witnessed after the murders and repeated her warning to Lizzie. The prosecution treated her account as evidence of an attempt to destroy incriminating clothing. Alice was a friend who had come to support the sisters, not a stranger with only a passing view of the household. Her appearance against Lizzie gave the testimony particular weight.

Emma took the stand for her sister. She said the dress had been old, faded, soiled, and spoiled by paint before the murders, and that disposing of it had been her suggestion. The defense also called a painter, who testified that paint had gotten on a dress during work at the property. That provided support for the explanation Alice had heard in the kitchen.

Robinson pressed the circumstances in which the garment had been destroyed. If Lizzie were concealing blood-stained clothing, he argued, why would she do it so openly, in the kitchen, in front of other people, with officers stationed outside? The prosecution saw bold destruction of evidence; the defense saw an ordinary act that looked suspicious only because it occurred during a murder inquiry.

The state did not substantiate its claim that the burned garment was the one Lizzie had worn during the killings. That left the jury to judge the opposing interpretations of an admitted event, rather than a demonstrated act of destroying the murderer’s clothes.

The trial thus gave the jurors more than one kind of conflict. Some witnesses disagreed over a person’s movements. Others disagreed about the meaning of something everyone accepted had occurred. Bridget’s account raised a question of location. The testimony about hostility addressed motive. Alice and Emma offered different understandings of the same kitchen event. The lawyers had to connect those separate matters into an argument about guilt.

Before the case went to the jury on June 20, Lizzie was allowed to address the court. She rose and said, “I am innocent. I leave it to my counsel to speak for me.” They were her only recorded words in the proceedings.

Justice Dewey’s final instructions emphasized the burden on the Commonwealth. The accusation rested on circumstantial evidence, and suspicion, probability, or conjecture could not sustain a guilty verdict. The jurors had to decide whether the necessary elements had been proved beyond a reasonable doubt. They were not being asked to select the most plausible story from the newspapers or to explain every unresolved detail of the morning.

The deliberation was short. Most accounts put it at about 90 minutes; others report less. Whatever the precise interval, the jury did not remain divided through a prolonged debate. Later that afternoon, the twelve men returned to the courtroom.

Asked whether they found Lizzie Borden guilty or not guilty of murdering Andrew and Abby, the foreman answered, “Not guilty.”

Lizzie bent forward, covered her face with her hands, and sobbed. Cheers and applause broke out in the gallery, loud enough to carry to the people outside. After months in jail and two weeks of trial, she had heard the words that ended the prosecution.

The Commonwealth had offered motive, opportunity, and suspicious circumstances, but it lacked an eyewitness, a confession, or a demonstrated connection between Lizzie and the acts of killing. Its permitted evidence had not proved the charges beyond a reasonable doubt.

The verdict was not proof of innocence, and it was not proof of anyone else’s guilt. It meant the state had failed to establish its accusation under the law. Chief Justice Mason discharged Lizzie, and the protection against double jeopardy barred Massachusetts from trying her again for the murders.

For the people still debating the case outside, questions remained. For Lizzie, the immediate consequence was simpler: she no longer had to return to the cell in Taunton. The capital charge under which she had been held was over. Her sister and lawyers were with her as she rose from the defense table and left the courtroom.

She passed the spectator benches, went down the courthouse steps, and walked out of New Bedford’s court a free woman.

The public story had begun departing from the record long before the trial. On October 10, 1892, while Lizzie was in jail, the Boston Globe published a front-page article by Henry Trickey claiming she had been pregnant and that Andrew had threatened to put her out of the house. The story supplied a private scandal and an immediate motive that readers could understand in a few lines.

It was fabricated. Two days later, the Globe printed a front-page retraction and apologized for the false accusation. This was not testimony that had failed to persuade a jury, or an honest disagreement about a witness’s memory. It was a story made from falsehoods and presented as news. Its publication gave an invented version of Lizzie’s life the prominence of a major newspaper’s front page.

A rhyme proved more durable than the article. It said Lizzie took an axe, gave her mother forty whacks, and then gave her father forty-one. The weapon was a hatchet, Abby was her stepmother, and the number of blows bore little relation to the medical findings. The verse also named the killer with a certainty no witness to the attacks had supplied. Its simple rhythm traveled farther than the testimony and attached itself to Lizzie’s name.

Films, plays, and television dramas continued reshaping the case over the following century, often making the woman and the house more familiar than the documented events.

Bridget Sullivan attracted stories of her own. After the trial, she left Fall River and eventually settled in Butte, Montana, away from the attention that had followed the household. Years later, reports circulated that she had made a deathbed admission before dying in 1948: that she had lied or changed parts of her testimony to help Lizzie. In some versions, she supposedly confided in her sister.

No written statement, sworn account, or contemporaneous documentation confirms that story. It emerged as secondhand rumor, not a recorded withdrawal of Bridget’s testimony. During her life, she did not publicly abandon the account she had given in court. The alleged confession cannot resolve whether she correctly identified the laughter at the top of the stairs. That disagreement survives without a verified later explanation from either woman.

Other stories tried to supply a different killer. John Morse drew attention because he arrived unexpectedly on the day before the murders and slept in the room where Abby died. He knew the family and had come to discuss property matters with Andrew. Those circumstances gave him a place in later theories.

But Morse’s movements after leaving the house were corroborated. He had gone to relatives across town, and witnesses supported his account. His recollections included streetcar details and people he had encountered. Police dismissed him as a suspect because his alibi held, not because his family connection had escaped their notice.

The outsider theory offered another possibility: an enemy from Andrew’s business life, a disgruntled tenant, or an unknown intruder. Andrew had dealings beyond the household, and Lizzie had spoken to Alice about people who might wish him harm. But no witness identified such an attacker entering or leaving, and no particular outsider was linked to the killings. The general possibility never became a supported accusation against a person.

More speculative accounts named William Borden as an illegitimate son seeking money, placed Emma secretly back in Fall River, proposed Bridget’s involvement, or invoked abuse. Records discredited the claim that William was Andrew’s son. The other proposed solutions likewise lacked the contemporary documentation, physical evidence, or verifiable testimony needed to establish them. Repetition over the years did not turn those allegations into events known to have occurred.

Lizzie remained the focus of suspicion for many people after the trial. The authorities never charged anyone else. No later prosecution brought a new witness to the attacks into court or supplied an established account of how they had happened.

For Lizzie and Emma, their father’s death also brought a material change. They inherited from an estate valued at about $300,000 and left Second Street. In the Hill district, where Andrew had declined to live, they bought a modern fourteen-room Victorian house and named it Maplecroft.

It was a very different home from the one where their father had kept his family. The sisters could now afford space and conveniences without seeking his approval. Yet Lizzie did not regain her former place in Fall River society. Old acquaintances avoided her, and the town largely shunned her. Much of her later life was spent privately at Maplecroft, with carriage rides and occasional trips away from Fall River.

Emma remained with her until 1905. Then, after a dispute neither sister publicly explained, she moved out and left the city. They never saw or spoke to each other again. Their separation became another part of Lizzie’s life on which later storytellers could speculate, but the sisters did not provide a public explanation.

Lizzie died of pneumonia at Maplecroft on June 1, 1927. She was 66. Emma died nine days later. Both were buried in the family plot at Oak Grove Cemetery in Fall River, near Andrew and Abby. The household whose relationships had been examined so closely was gone.

The house on Second Street survived. It is now a museum and historic inn. Visitors can enter the rooms, see the sitting room, and climb the front stairs. The layout that once complicated ordinary movement through the home remains something a person can experience directly: the separate routes upstairs, the rooms opening into one another, and the guest bedroom beside the front landing.

Among all the people associated with that house, Abby is often remembered least. Her place in the story became a relationship to be clarified, a count of wounds, or a line in a rhyme. She was the first victim, but public attention settled on the surviving daughter and the trial that followed.

Abby had been married to Andrew for nearly three decades. Whatever the disputed accounts tell us about the household’s divisions, much of her life there consisted of keeping it running. On the morning she died, a guest had stayed overnight and his room needed attention.

At about nine on August 4, Abby went up the front staircase to make the bed John Morse had used. Andrew was leaving for his business downtown. Morse had gone to call on relatives. Bridget had windows to wash. Abby’s task was upstairs, in the room beside the landing.

She was preparing the guest room, an ordinary chore in a house that had begun its day. She never came back down.
