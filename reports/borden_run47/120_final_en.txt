[[ACT:act1]]

On the morning of August 4, 1892, Andrew Borden came home to Second Street in Fall River, Massachusetts, and tried his key in the front door. The lock wouldn't give. Inside, the family's maid, Bridget Sullivan, heard him at the entrance and came to let him in.

As Bridget struggled with the lock, she heard someone laugh. The sound, she said, came from the top of the front staircase, beside the guest bedroom. She recognized the voice as Andrew's younger daughter, Lizzie.

Lizzie denied it. She said she hadn't laughed and hadn't been standing upstairs. When her father returned, she insisted, she was on the ground floor.

Bridget placed Lizzie on the landing above the entrance. Lizzie placed herself downstairs. Within an hour, that difference would concern more than where a daughter had been standing when her father came home.

A little after eleven, Bridget was in her third-floor room, resting after washing windows, when Lizzie called up to her: "Come down quickly. Father is dead. Someone came in and killed him."

Bridget hurried downstairs and found Lizzie near the back entrance. Next door, Adelaide Churchill noticed Lizzie at the doorway, looking distressed. Mrs. Churchill called across the yard to ask what was wrong.

"Oh, Mrs. Churchill, do come over," Lizzie answered. "Someone has killed father."

Mrs. Churchill crossed the yard and entered the house. In the downstairs sitting room, 70-year-old Andrew Borden lay stretched out on the sofa. At first glance, his position might have suggested that he was resting after his morning walk. But someone had struck him repeatedly in the face and head with a hatchet. His skull was fractured, his features almost unrecognizable. Blood covered the sofa cushions and had spattered the wall behind him.

Andrew had gone downtown that morning to conduct business. He had returned to his own home, passed through the front entrance, and lain down in a room the household used every day. Now his daughter was calling neighbors for help, and the maid who had opened the door for him was confronting the aftermath of an attack she said she had neither seen nor heard.

There was still another member of the family to find: Andrew's wife, Abby.

Bridget and Mrs. Churchill went to the front staircase and started up toward the second floor. The guest bedroom stood beside the landing where Bridget said she had heard Lizzie laughing. Inside, between the bed and the dressing bureau, Abby Borden lay face down on the carpet.

She, too, had been struck repeatedly in the head. Blood had pooled beneath her body. Abby was 64.

The search for Andrew's wife had uncovered another murder. The violence was not confined to the sitting room, and the emergency was no longer the death of one man. Two people had been killed in separate rooms of the same house, while the surviving women said they knew nothing of either attack.

Outside was a busy manufacturing city on Mount Hope Bay. Fall River's textile mills and commercial streets surrounded a household that kept itself closely shut off from the neighborhood. The wooden house at 92 Second Street stood only a block from the commercial center. Its exterior doors were routinely locked, even with the city going about its business nearby.

This was not an isolated property far from possible witnesses. Neighbors lived close enough to notice Lizzie at the back entrance and hear her call. The distance Mrs. Churchill crossed to offer help was a yard, not a country road.

At the time of the killings, the only people known to be in the house besides the victims were Lizzie and Bridget. Lizzie was 32, unmarried, and living with her father and stepmother. Bridget was 26 and had worked for the family for three years. Neither reported seeing an attacker.

There was no sign of a forced door or window. The rooms had not been ransacked, and no missing valuables suggested a robbery. Whoever was responsible had moved through an occupied home in daylight without being identified by either woman.

Shortly after a quarter past eleven, a telephone call from a neighboring stable reached the Fall River police. Marshal Rufus Hilliard received word that Andrew Borden had been killed. The department was shorthanded. August 4 was the day of its annual picnic at Rocky Point, and many officers were away from the city.

Officer George Allen was dispatched to Second Street. He reached the house around twenty past eleven, as word of the killing was spreading along the block. Neighbors were already gathering near the property. More officers followed, entering a house where people had come to help and where two rooms now had to be examined as murder scenes.

The reduced police force had to establish who belonged there, who had arrived after the alarm, and what each person could say about the morning. Downstairs was Andrew's body on the sofa. Upstairs was Abby's body beside the bed. Between them were the rooms, doors, and staircases through which the household had continued its ordinary movements.

The locked doors did not establish that an outsider couldn't have entered. But no witness reported seeing an unfamiliar person come in or escape. For the officers, the first task was not to choose an explanation. It was to recover a sequence from the people who had been there.

One disagreement was already waiting for them. Bridget said Lizzie had been at the top of the front stairs when Andrew came home. Lizzie said she had been downstairs. It was a short distance within a modest house, but it placed her either beside the guest bedroom or away from it.

The investigation would carry that disagreement into court. No one was ever convicted, and the murders have never been officially solved.

[[ACT:act2]]

Lizzie Andrew Borden was born in Fall River on July 19, 1860. By the summer of 1892, she had just turned 32. Much of her public life revolved around the Central Congregational Church, where she taught Sunday school, took part in religious societies, and volunteered through church committees and local hospital work.

To people who knew her through those activities, she was a woman from an established family doing the charitable and religious work expected of someone in her position. Her life was rooted in Fall River. She was unmarried, had no occupation outside those commitments, and still lived in the house her father maintained on Second Street.

Her older sister, Emma, was 41. Their mother, Sarah, had died when Lizzie was a child, and Emma had often looked after her younger sister. Described as quiet and reserved, Emma shared Lizzie's unmarried life at home. When the murders occurred, she was visiting friends in Fairhaven, about 15 miles away.

Their father, Andrew Jackson Borden, had built a substantial fortune without adopting the way of life that usually accompanied one. He began as a furniture maker, producing furniture and caskets. Over the years, he expanded into real estate, textile mill investments, and banking. By 1892, he was president of the Union Savings Bank, and his estate was estimated at about $300,000.

Other wealthy Fall River families lived in the fashionable district called The Hill. Andrew kept his family near the mills and downtown businesses, in a modest wooden house without modern conveniences such as indoor plumbing. His frugality was well known. The family had money, but Andrew's decisions governed how much of its comfort they enjoyed.

His second wife, Abby Durfee Gray, had married him in June 1865, roughly two years after Sarah's death. Abby had been part of the household for nearly three decades. She had managed the home through most of Lizzie's life, but those years had not made her relationship with Andrew's daughters simple or secure.

Money and property were among the reported sources of friction. Accounts published after the murders described a dispute around 1887, when Andrew bought a house for Abby's half-sister to occupy. According to those reports, Lizzie and Emma objected to family money benefiting Abby's relatives. Lizzie reportedly argued that whatever Andrew did for his wife's people, he should do for his own children.

That feud became an important part of the proposed motive for murder. Its details, however, came through accounts that were not equally well supported. The property transactions are firmer ground than every conversation later said to have surrounded them.

Andrew transferred houses to his daughters for the nominal price of $1 each. Several years later, Lizzie and Emma sold the properties back to him for $5,000. The arrangement gave the sisters money of their own, though both remained under their father's roof and financially dependent on him. It settled a transaction without necessarily settling their concerns about the eventual division of his much larger estate.

Accounts of the women's daily relations differed sharply. Some acquaintances described a cold household, with the daughters taking meals separately rather than joining Andrew and Abby. Witnesses later said Lizzie believed Abby had married for money. Other accounts, including Lizzie's formal testimony, described their everyday dealings as cordial.

Those versions cannot be reduced to a single reliable portrait of life at Second Street. There were reports of resentment, and there were denials of open conflict. The family had lived together for years, while the descriptions that became most influential were collected after two of its members were dead.

One statement from the morning of the murders is documented. When an officer referred to Abby as Lizzie's mother, Lizzie corrected him: "She is not my mother, sir. She is my father's wife. My mother died when I was a child."

Lizzie and Emma called her Mrs. Borden. The correction preserved a distinction the daughters had maintained for years: Abby was married to their father, but they did not address her as their mother.

The house itself allowed its occupants to live at close quarters while keeping parts of their lives separate. Originally built as a multi-family dwelling, it lacked an upstairs hallway connecting the front rooms to the rear. Rooms opened directly into one another, and some connecting doors were kept fastened.

A door stood between Lizzie's bedroom and the bedroom occupied by Andrew and Abby, but it was bolted on both sides. Without opening that barrier, Lizzie could not simply step across the upper floor to reach her father. She had to go down the front staircase, cross the ground floor through the sitting room and dining room, and climb the back stairs from the kitchen.

The front stairs served Lizzie's room and the guest bedroom. The rear stairs led toward Andrew and Abby's room and the domestic quarters above. Someone could be only a room away upstairs yet have to travel through much of the house to reach another occupant. That layout would matter whenever investigators tried to follow a witness's account from one place to the next.

Bridget Sullivan slept on the third floor. An Irish domestic servant, she had lived and worked with the Bordens for three years. With Emma away, the regular household consisted of Bridget, Lizzie, Andrew, and Abby.

Then, on Wednesday, August 3, an unexpected visitor arrived.

John Vinnicum Morse was Sarah Borden's brother, making him Lizzie and Emma's maternal uncle. He had spent much of his adult life farming in Iowa and did not often visit the family. He arrived unannounced that afternoon while in the East on personal business. Andrew invited him to stay for several days so they could discuss property matters and investments.

Morse was given the guest bedroom at the top of the front staircase. A room that normally stood available for visitors was occupied on the night before the murders, and Abby would have a bed to make there the following morning.

The household was already unsettled by illness. In the days before August 4, Andrew, Abby, and Bridget had suffered severe stomach trouble. Mutton left out in the summer heat over several days appears to have caused food poisoning, but some members of the household feared something deliberate. Andrew suspected someone might have tried to poison the family.

That evening, between seven and nine, Lizzie visited her friend Alice Russell. Alice later described her as anxious and depressed. Lizzie spoke of an unknown threat hanging over the family and said she feared something terrible would happen. She mentioned her father's business affairs and suggested that he might have enemies who wished him harm.

She did not identify an attacker or describe a particular plan. Alice heard those fears against the background of sickness and unease in the house. The significance of the conversation changed after the following morning, when a daughter's foreboding became something investigators wanted to understand.

Another account concerned an encounter said to have taken place earlier on August 3. At a local pharmacy, a woman matching Lizzie's description reportedly asked to buy prussic acid, or hydrogen cyanide, an extremely deadly poison.

The clerk refused to sell it without a prescription. The woman said she wanted it to clean a sealskin cape. He refused again. Pharmacy staff later supported the account of the exchange and the identification.

Lizzie denied entering the shop or trying to buy poison. Her attorneys maintained that another woman had been mistaken for her. The pharmacy witnesses stood by their identification, but no sale had taken place, and the competing accounts were never conclusively reconciled.

By the end of that Wednesday, John Morse was staying in the guest room, the family had been ill, and Alice had heard Lizzie speak of danger. Beside those established circumstances stood a disputed accusation: that someone believed to be Lizzie had tried to obtain a lethal poison on the day before the killings.

[[ACT:act3]]

The household began moving early on Thursday, August 4. Accounts differ about who shared breakfast. Some place Andrew, Abby, John Morse, and Bridget at the table around seven, with Lizzie still upstairs. Others have Lizzie joining her parents, or coming downstairs later to eat alone.

The departures are clearer. Around a quarter to nine, Morse left to visit relatives across town, intending to return by noon. Andrew went out around nine to conduct business at the banks and in the commercial district.

Abby went upstairs to straighten the guest bedroom and make the bed Morse had used. That was the last time any witness confirmed seeing her alive.

Downstairs, Lizzie mentioned a sale on dress goods at a local department store and suggested Bridget might want to go shopping. Bridget, still feeling unwell after the household's stomach illness, declined. She stayed at the house to wash the ground-floor windows.

Andrew returned around half past ten and found the front door fastened. Bridget let him in. He later lay down on the sitting-room sofa. A little after eleven, Lizzie raised the alarm, and the search that followed uncovered both bodies.

The physicians examining Andrew found a concentrated attack on his face and head. The force had fractured his skull and facial bones, severed his nose, and split one eye. He had been struck from above and behind while lying on the sofa. There were no signs that he had managed to raise a hand in defense.

Abby's wounds were concentrated mainly on the back of her head and neck. Medical accounts described an initial strike to the side of her face, above the ear, followed by repeated blows after she fell forward. She lay in the narrow space between the guest-room bed and the bureau, where she had been attending to the room.

Reported counts vary. Medical examiner Dr. William Dolan recorded ten head wounds on Andrew; other accounts give eleven blows. For Abby, the usual counts are 18 or 19, with Dolan recording 18 wounds to her head and one to her back. The variations do not change the nature of the attacks: both victims were struck repeatedly at close range with a sharp, heavy instrument.

The condition of the bodies established something more consequential than the exact tally. Upstairs, Abby was cold to the touch. The blood beneath her was dark, coagulated, and dry. Downstairs, Andrew's body was still warm, and liquid blood remained at his fresh wounds.

Abby had died first.

That conclusion was clear to the doctors, including Dolan. Determining exactly how much earlier she had died was more difficult. The coroner placed her death between nine and half past ten, with several assessments favoring about half past nine. Andrew's death was estimated between half past ten and ten past eleven.

Individual medical opinions suggested an interval of an hour to an hour and a half, while some allowed nearly two hours. These were estimates drawn from the bodies and the accounts of the morning, not times fixed by a witness watching an attack. But there was no scientific doubt that a substantial interval separated the deaths.

While Andrew was out conducting business, his wife had been killed upstairs. The discovery of her body did not occur until after he had returned and been killed below. A person responsible for both attacks had remained undetected in or around the property between them, while the household's ordinary movements continued.

Police searched for an implement capable of making the wounds. In the cellar, among coal bins and stored tools, they found two axes and two intact hatchets. Another object drew particular attention: a hatchet head in a box of old tools, with most of its handle missing. A short stub of wood remained in the opening where the handle had fitted.

Several officers thought the break looked fresh. That observation gave the discarded tool an importance it might not otherwise have had. A recently broken handle could suggest recent handling, and the prosecution would argue that the wood had been removed because it could have absorbed blood.

The head's blade measured roughly three and a half inches across. Prosecutors maintained that its dimensions matched the injuries to the victims' skulls. Under their theory, the killer had broken off the handle and covered the metal head in ash or fine cellar dust to make it look as though it had been lying there undisturbed.

But the officers did not agree about the break. One contradicted the claim that it was recent, saying the wood showed no fresh snap. The condition that made the hatchet suspicious to some investigators did not look suspicious to another.

The ash also had two possible explanations. The prosecution treated it as a disguise applied after the killings. Defense testimony described a large accumulation of ash in the cellar that coated the surrounding objects, making a thick layer on an abandoned tool consistent with ordinary storage.

Chemical examination did not establish conclusive traces of blood on the blade. The hatchet head became the proposed murder weapon, not a weapon conclusively identified as having been used in either attack. Its dimensions supported the prosecution's argument, while the disputed handle and the surrounding ash supported the defense's answer.

Elsewhere in the cellar, in the washroom, officers found a pail containing pieces of cloth soaked in blood. In a house where investigators were looking for signs of an assailant's clothing or an effort to clean up, the discovery required an explanation.

Lizzie said the cloths were connected with her menstrual period. Her physician, Dr. Seabury Bowen, confirmed that she was menstruating. His statement supported that circumstance; it was not a scientific identification of the blood on each piece of cloth.

Accounts of the discovery and its handling vary in detail. Investigative accounts and later reviews generally describe the officers as accepting the explanation and leaving the rags without chemical examination. Some say the pail was noted and left in the cellar. The surviving descriptions do not supply a completed test that established whose blood it contained.

The handling reflected both the limited biological testing available in 1892 and the social discomfort surrounding menstruation. Police could examine visible stains and use the chemical tests of their period, but they had no blood typing or reliable way to pursue microscopic traces and connect them to a particular person. In the case of the pail, an explanation from Lizzie and confirmation of her period from the family doctor took the place of further examination.

Other searches produced no hidden, blood-drenched clothing. No missing money or jewelry supplied a robbery motive, and no damaged lock or broken window established a forced entry. Those findings left the officers with an extensive search but no direct physical connection between a suspect and either act of killing.

The contrast was stark. The violence had left unmistakable evidence in two rooms: broken bone, blood on furnishings, and two bodies whose condition revealed the order of the attacks. Yet the objects that might have led away from those rooms to a person were less decisive. The suspected weapon was contested. The bloodied cloths had a domestic explanation and had not been conclusively examined. The searches did not uncover an assailant's clothing.

More than an hour appears to have separated the killings. During the morning, the people known to have been on the premises were Lizzie, Bridget, and, before his departure, John Morse, along with the victims. None of the surviving witnesses reported seeing the violence.

The physical evidence could not place a weapon in a particular person's hands or establish whether the killer belonged to the household or had come from outside. To go further, investigators had to turn to the people who had been there: what they said they had done, what they said they had heard, and how those accounts matched the house.

For Lizzie, that meant explaining two absences. Where had she been when Andrew was attacked? And why had she believed Abby was somewhere other than the guest room upstairs?

[[ACT:act4]]

In the hours immediately following the murders, officers questioned Lizzie inside the house where Andrew and Abby still lay dead. They wanted a chronology from the only family member who had been on the property.

Lizzie's account to police began with a message. Earlier that morning, she said, a messenger had brought Abby a handwritten note at the front door asking her to visit a sick friend. Abby had gone to answer the summons. Bridget was washing windows, Andrew was downtown, and nothing had happened to alert Lizzie to trouble.

When Andrew came home around half past ten, Lizzie told him about the note and the visit. He settled on the sitting-room sofa. She then went outside to the loft in the barn, she told officers, to look for a piece of iron to repair a window screen.

She estimated that she had been away for about 20 minutes. Returning to the house through the screen door shortly after eleven, she entered the sitting room and found her father dead. In the sequence she described, an intruder must have attacked Andrew while she was outside.

Lizzie did not claim to have seen anyone enter or leave. Her account explained where she said she had been during the attack, while placing Abby away from the house and Bridget at her work. Police now had to check the details of those movements.

Several officers were troubled by Lizzie's manner as well as her words. They saw no tears or obvious panic from a daughter who had just discovered her father so violently killed. She answered their questions directly and kept an even temper throughout the exchanges.

Others read that composure as shock, or as an effort to maintain her dignity. The disagreement over her behavior began almost immediately. What the officers could investigate more directly was the message that supposedly explained Abby's absence.

Marshal Rufus Hilliard and his men searched the house from cellar to attic for the note. They looked through places where a message might have been put down or discarded, including tables, mantels, and waste receptacles. Nothing was found.

Lizzie suggested Abby might have burned it. Outside the house, inquiries produced no sick friend who said she had sent for Mrs. Borden. No messenger was identified. The summons Lizzie had mentioned to both Bridget and Andrew remained unconfirmed.

After Andrew's body was discovered, Lizzie directed attention back upstairs. She told Mrs. Churchill and Bridget that she believed she had heard Abby come in and suggested someone look for her on the second floor.

That was different from the account in which Abby was away visiting a sick friend. Lizzie offered a return as the explanation, but police could verify neither the original summons nor the departure. Upstairs, Abby's body showed that she had been dead for a considerable time. The search for a living woman who might have come home had instead found someone who had been killed before Andrew returned.

Another detail in Lizzie's account concerned her father's preparations to rest. She said she had helped him remove his shoes. In versions of the story, he then put on slippers before lying down. Some accounts also associate a mention of slippers with Bridget's recollection.

But Andrew was found wearing his outdoor boots.

The heavy, lace-up walking boots remained on his feet as he lay on the sofa. The photographs of the sitting room recorded them, with his feet resting against the cushions. He had not died in slippers, and there were no discarded boots beside the couch to support the change of footwear described in Lizzie's account. No slippers were found on the floor nearby. He was still dressed in his street clothes.

To the investigators, that discrepancy was particularly troubling because it concerned an action she said she had helped perform. It was not merely a sound heard from another room or an estimate of how long an errand had taken. The police suspected that the account of helping Andrew settle down had supplied an invented picture of an uneventful morning. That was their interpretation of the mismatch between her words and the scene.

The question of Lizzie's own clothing presented a different problem. When neighbors and officers arrived, they saw no visible blood on her face, hands, or hair. The dress she was wearing appeared clean.

Bridget remembered Lizzie coming downstairs that morning in a blue dress. The first police inspection of the house, however, was cursory. Officers did not seize Lizzie's wardrobe or examine all her personal effects in detail. They had no garment set aside as the clothing she had worn throughout the critical hours.

The attacks had scattered blood across walls, furniture, and carpet. Investigators wanted to know whether the person responsible had changed clothes or hidden something stained. Bridget's recollection gave them a color, but it did not establish which dress should be examined.

Three days later, Alice Russell saw an event that would make that question central to the prosecution.

Alice was staying at Second Street to help support the sisters after the murders. On Sunday, August 7, at about eleven in the morning, she went into the kitchen and found Lizzie beside the stove with a dress.

It was a cotton Bedford cord gown, light blue with small dark figures. Alice recognized it as an everyday morning dress Lizzie had worn earlier that spring. Now Lizzie was tearing it into strips and preparing to put the pieces into the fire.

Alice asked what she was doing. Lizzie said she was going to burn the old dress because it was covered with paint.

Emma was in the room. She confirmed that she had advised Lizzie to get rid of it because it was no longer fit to wear. The explanation both sisters offered was that the garment had already been spoiled, not that anything had happened to it on the morning of the murders.

Alice nevertheless saw a danger in the act. Police officers were stationed outside the house, and the sisters were living under intense public scrutiny. Destroying clothing at that moment was something others could interpret very differently.

"If I were you I wouldn't let anybody see me do that, Lizzie," she said.

Lizzie did not argue, but she continued. She fed the torn fabric into the kitchen stove until the garment was consumed.

The burning took place in a shared room with Emma and Alice present. Lizzie had not waited for a private moment when neither woman could see her. That openness would later become part of the defense's argument. To Alice, it was troubling enough that she had warned her friend while it was happening.

Her account preserved a specific event: a recognizable dress, Lizzie's explanation about paint, Emma's agreement, and the warning at the stove. It did not give Alice a continuous view of what Lizzie had worn three days earlier. Bridget's memory concerned a blue dress on Thursday; Alice's concerned a particular light blue dress in the kitchen on Sunday.

The distinction mattered because the fabric was now gone. A comparison could no longer begin with an actual garment laid out before investigators. They would have to rely on the witnesses' recollections and on whatever others knew about the paint.

Nobody could establish whether the dress burned on Sunday was the one Lizzie had worn on the morning of August 4. It might have been an old paint-stained gown, as she and Emma said. Its destruction might have removed something investigators had reason to preserve. But neither its fabric nor its stains could ever be examined.

What remained was Alice Russell's memory of Lizzie standing at the kitchen stove, tearing a dress into strips, and continuing after her friend told her not to let anyone see.

For a week after the murders, Lizzie remained at home. Emma returned from Fairhaven, and Alice Russell stayed with the sisters. Outside, reporters, neighbors, and curious townspeople gathered along Second Street. Police watched the property and observed movements around the house.

Inside, the women continued living in the rooms that had become the subject of questions across the country. Meals still had to be prepared, and the ordinary necessities of the household continued under scrutiny. Lizzie slept in her upstairs bedroom while officers sought an account of what had happened nearby.

The authorities had not arrested her, but they made their suspicions known. On the evening of Saturday, August 6, Mayor John Coughlin and the lead investigator went to the house and told Lizzie she was considered a suspect in the homicides.

They did not take her into custody that night. She remained there through the following days, including the Sunday morning when Alice saw the dress destroyed.

On Tuesday, August 9, five days after the killings, officials opened a coroner's inquest on the upper floor of the Fall River police station. District Attorney Hosea Knowlton had consulted with local officials in Marshal Hilliard's office before the inquiry began.

The proceedings were closed to the public and press. Witnesses entered individually to give their accounts under oath. Bridget was among the first called.

An inquest was an investigation, not a trial, but the answers given there could have serious consequences. Lizzie entered without an attorney beside her. Under the procedure then in use, her family lawyer, Andrew Jackson Jennings, was not permitted to accompany her into the room.

She would have to hear Knowlton's questions and formulate her answers without Jennings there to advise her, challenge a question, or help clarify what was being asked.

There was also a medical circumstance. In the aftermath of the murders, Dr. Bowen had prescribed regular doses of morphine for Lizzie's nerves. According to accounts of the proceedings, she remained under the medication's influence when she testified.

Across sessions from August 9 through August 11, she faced repeated questioning about the morning. This would be the only occasion on which she gave a sworn account from the witness stand of the events of August 4.

Knowlton pressed her to locate herself when Andrew returned. Her answers changed. At first she placed herself on the stairs. Then she said she had been downstairs in the kitchen. At another point, she described being in the yard eating pears.

The examination returned to the front entrance and to what she had been doing while Bridget worked the lock. Instead of fixing her position more firmly, the questions produced incompatible locations.

The account of the barn errand changed as well. In her first statement to police, Lizzie had said she went to find iron or tin to repair a window screen. At the inquest, she described looking for lead sinkers for a fishing trip she planned to take with Emma in Fairhaven.

Asked what had kept her in the barn for about 20 minutes, she spoke of lingering to eat pears in the heat. Knowlton was trying to follow the absence in which she said her father had been attacked. The object she had gone to fetch, and the way she had spent the time, no longer matched the first explanation.

Over the three days, her descriptions of going upstairs, hearing sounds, and moving between rooms did not yield a consistent route. Confronted with differences between what she had said and what she was now saying, Lizzie answered, "I don't know what I have said."

Accounts describe her manner as erratic at times. Some replies seemed evasive to her questioners; others were resistant. Lizzie objected to the way she was being examined: "Some of your questions I have difficulty answering because I don't know just how you mean them."

To investigators, the shifting answers looked like an account coming apart under pressure. But the transcript does not establish what lay behind them. Deliberate evasion, grief, prescribed morphine, and the strain of answering without counsel offered competing explanations for her performance. The contradictory words survived; the reason for them could not be read directly from the page.

The inquiry had produced no admission. It had also failed to reconcile Lizzie's movements. Officials who had summoned her to explain the morning now had a sworn record that strengthened their decision to bring charges.

On the afternoon of Thursday, August 11, as the third day's proceedings ended, Marshal Hilliard executed a warrant for her arrest. One week after the killings, Lizzie Borden was taken into custody and charged with the murders of her father and stepmother.

She was charged, not convicted. Brought before the district court, she pleaded not guilty. She was then transferred to the county jail in Taunton, 20 miles north of Fall River, to await further proceedings.

The change was immediate and practical. Until then, the police had watched her at home. Now she was confined away from Emma and the house where the investigation had begun. The questions that had surrounded her daily life became the work of lawyers preparing for the next legal decision.

Nearly four months passed before the grand jury acted. During that period, the prosecution's case remained one built from circumstances and statements. The arrest identified the person authorities intended to prosecute; the grand jury would decide whether the accusation should proceed to trial.

In early December, Alice Russell appeared before that body and described what she had witnessed at the kitchen stove. Her account brought the destruction of the dress into the formal case. Prosecutors could present it as possible concealment after the murders, separate from the conflicting recollections of the morning itself.

Alice had stayed with the sisters as a friend. Her willingness to describe what she had seen gave the state testimony from inside the household during the days following the deaths.

On December 2, 1892, the grand jury indicted Lizzie Borden for murder. The case was sent to the Superior Court, with a trial set for the following June in New Bedford.

Lizzie remained in Taunton through the winter and spring while the lawyers prepared. Knowlton had to turn the investigation into a case that could be presented under the rules of a criminal trial. Jennings and the defense would challenge not only the conclusions officials had drawn, but the way some of their evidence had been obtained.

At the center of that coming dispute was Lizzie's inquest testimony. Its contradictions had helped lead to her arrest. Yet it had been taken in a closed inquiry, without her lawyer present, while she was receiving morphine prescribed by her physician.

Before a jury could weigh those words, the court would have to decide whether the jurors were allowed to hear them at all.

[[ACT:act6]]

Lizzie Borden's trial opened on June 5, 1893, in New Bedford, Massachusetts, a textile city 20 miles south of Fall River. She had spent ten months in custody. Reporters from Boston and New York crowded the press benches, telegraph dispatches carried developments across the country, and spectators filled the gallery and gathered outside the courthouse.

The Sunday school teacher from Fall River was now a defendant facing a capital charge. The prosecution sought a conviction for the murders of two people in the home where she had lived. Her defense had to answer the case permitted in court, while the public followed a much larger collection of allegations.

The jury consisted of twelve men, as Massachusetts law did not permit women to serve. Farmers, tradesmen, and businessmen would decide the accusation that had divided opinion well beyond the county.

Andrew Jennings represented Lizzie alongside George D. Robinson, a former governor of Massachusetts whose courtroom experience and public standing brought considerable authority to the defense. The family's wealth had enabled Lizzie to retain lawyers with both detailed knowledge of her circumstances and the resources to contest the state's case.

She sat beside them in black, holding a lace handkerchief and smelling salts. For much of the trial, she maintained the quiet composure officers had remarked on after the killings. But the presentation of the victims' skulls overwhelmed her.

Removed and cleaned during the autopsies, the skulls were brought into court to show the precise dimensions of the hatchet strikes. Confronted with her father's fractured skull, Lizzie fainted in her chair. Attendants revived her before the proceedings could continue.

The public could see that moment. Some of the most consequential decisions, however, concerned evidence the jury would never see or hear.

Jennings and Robinson challenged the transcript of the inquest. They argued that Lizzie's statements had been improperly obtained during questioning without counsel, also pointing to her prescribed morphine. The justices excluded the record because she had been questioned without a lawyer.

The contradictory sworn account that had helped lead investigators to arrest her would not be placed before the jury.

Lizzie did not testify at trial. She was entitled to remain silent, and the prosecution could not require her to take the stand. The jurors therefore did not hear her examined about her changing locations or the different purposes she had given for the barn errand. Her defense would be conducted through other witnesses and her lawyers' arguments.

The pharmacy accusation faced a separate challenge. Knowlton wanted clerk Eli Bence to describe the alleged attempt to buy prussic acid on August 3. The prosecution argued that the request suggested preparation for murder and an earlier effort to obtain a lethal substance.

The defense objected that neither Andrew nor Abby had died of poison. The alleged encounter was disputed, and the defense argued that it was irrelevant to a trial for the attacks that had killed them. The justices sustained the objection.

The jury did not hear the pharmacist's testimony. Knowlton could not use the reported purchase attempt to establish prior intent. A story that had become closely associated with the case outside court was absent from the evidence on which the jurors were asked to decide it.

Bridget did take the stand. Her testimony returned the courtroom to Andrew's arrival at the front door and the sound she said she heard above her.

She supplied an explanation for the laughter. While struggling with the lock, she had muttered a curse in frustration. She believed Lizzie had laughed at that slip of profanity from the landing.

That context changed the opening detail. Bridget was not describing laughter at something she knew had happened in the guest room. She understood it as a response to her own difficulty at the door. But her account still placed Lizzie upstairs, and Lizzie still denied being there or laughing.

To establish motive, Knowlton called witnesses who described resentment within the household. Acquaintances and a dressmaker spoke of an unhappy home and said Lizzie had expressed hatred for Abby. Her refusal to call Abby her mother formed part of that portrait.

The prosecution sought to connect the family divisions to the killings. The defense presented the woman known through church and charitable work, while witnesses for the state described statements made in less public settings. The jurors had to judge the testimony without mistaking either a respectable reputation or a report of hostility for an eyewitness account of murder.

Alice Russell gave the prosecution testimony about an event she had actually observed. She described the destruction of the dress and repeated the warning she had given Lizzie. The state treated her account as evidence of an effort to eliminate incriminating clothing.

Alice was not a stranger who had glimpsed the household from outside. She was a friend who had come to help the sisters after the deaths. Her presence in the kitchen placed her close to an event the prosecution considered crucial, even though she could not supply the connection to the killings that prosecutors wanted the jury to infer.

Emma testified for her sister. She said the gown had been old, faded, soiled, and ruined by paint weeks before the murders. Getting rid of it, she told the jury, had been her suggestion. The defense also called a painter, who confirmed that paint had gotten on a dress during work at the property.

Robinson emphasized the circumstances. If Lizzie were concealing blood-stained clothing, he argued, why destroy it so openly, in a shared kitchen, in front of other people, with police officers outside? What the prosecution described as bold destruction of evidence, the defense presented as disposal of an old garment.

The state did not substantiate its claim that the dress was the one worn during the killings. The jury heard the event and the opposing explanations, but not proof that the garment had been the murderer's clothing.

For Knowlton, these separate strands had to form a convincing whole: opportunity inside the house, testimony about resentment, the disputed location on the stairs, and conduct afterward that he regarded as concealment. The defense challenged both the individual claims and the connections between them.

The inquest record could not supply Lizzie's contradictory sworn answers. The pharmacy account could not supply the alleged prior attempt to obtain poison. The case reaching the jury was narrower than the accusation many newspaper readers had followed for months.

Before the case went to the jury on June 20, Lizzie was allowed to address the court. She rose and said, "I am innocent. I leave it to my counsel to speak for me."

They were her only recorded words in the trial.

Justice Justin Dewey delivered the final instructions. He emphasized that the Commonwealth's case rested on circumstantial evidence. Suspicion, probability, or conjecture could not sustain a guilty verdict. The prosecution had to establish the necessary elements beyond a reasonable doubt.

The jurors withdrew to deliberate. Court watchers and reporters expected a long, contentious debate behind closed doors. After the weeks of attention and conflicting testimony, their deliberation was brief. Most accounts put it at about 90 minutes; others report less.

At half past three, word spread that a decision had been reached. Spectators pressed into the gallery. The twelve men returned to their places, and the court asked whether they found Lizzie Borden guilty or not guilty of murdering Andrew and Abby.

The foreman answered, "Not guilty."

Lizzie bent forward, buried her face in her hands, and sobbed. Cheers, shouts, and applause broke out in the gallery, loud enough to be heard outside the building. The audience's restraint gave way as the decision that ended the prosecution was announced.

The Commonwealth had offered motive, opportunity, and suspicious circumstances, but it lacked an eyewitness, a confession, or a demonstrated connection between Lizzie and the acts of killing. Its permitted evidence had not proved the charges beyond a reasonable doubt.

Chief Justice Albert Mason discharged her. Ten months of confinement in Taunton were over, and the capital charge no longer held her. The charge had carried the penalty of death.

The verdict was not proof of innocence, and it was not proof of anyone else's guilt. It meant the state had failed to establish its accusation under the law. The protection against double jeopardy barred Massachusetts from trying Lizzie again for these murders.

Emma, Jennings, and Robinson were with her as she rose from the defense table. She passed the spectator benches and left the courtroom where she had listened to two weeks of testimony about her family and her life.

Then she went down the courthouse steps into the June afternoon. She did not have to return to the cell in Taunton. Lizzie Borden walked out of court a free woman.

The public story had begun departing from the record long before the trial. Newspapers competing for readers published lurid dispatches that mixed investigative facts with rumor. On October 10, 1892, while Lizzie was in jail, the Boston Globe published a front-page article by Henry Trickey claiming she had been pregnant and that Andrew had threatened to put her out of the house.

It supplied a private scandal and an immediate motive in a few dramatic lines. It was fabricated.

Two days later, the Globe printed a front-page retraction and apologized for the false accusation. This was not a disputed witness identification or a recollection that had changed under questioning. It was a hoax presented as news, giving an invented version of Lizzie's life the authority and prominence of a major newspaper's front page.

Children chanted it while skipping rope, and newspapers repeated it across the country. It said Lizzie took an axe, gave her mother forty whacks, and then gave her father forty-one. The weapon was a hatchet, Abby was her stepmother, and the verse's 81 blows bore little relation to the medical findings, which recorded roughly 29 strikes in all. The verse also named the killer with a certainty no witness to the attacks had supplied.

Over the following century, films, plays, and television dramas repeatedly reimagined the woman and the house.

Bridget Sullivan attracted stories of her own. After the trial, she left Fall River and eventually settled in the mining town of Butte, Montana, where she spent the rest of her life away from the attention that had followed the household.

Years later, reports circulated that she had made a deathbed admission before dying in 1948: that she had lied or changed parts of her testimony to help Lizzie. In some versions, she supposedly confided in her sister.

It survives as secondhand rumor, not a recorded withdrawal of Bridget's testimony. During her life, she did not publicly abandon the account she had given in court. The disagreement over the laughter at the top of the stairs remains unresolved.

Other stories tried to supply a different killer. John Morse drew attention because he arrived unexpectedly on the day before the murders and slept in the room where Abby died. He knew the family and had come to discuss property matters with Andrew.

But witnesses corroborated his movements after he left the house to visit relatives. His recollections included streetcar schedules, numbers on transit workers' caps, and people he had encountered. Police dismissed him as a suspect because his alibi held.

An outside attacker offered another possibility: an enemy from Andrew's business life, a disgruntled tenant, or an unknown intruder. Lizzie herself had spoken to Alice about people who might wish her father harm. That theory required someone to reach Abby upstairs, remain hidden in the compact house for over an hour, and then kill Andrew before leaving. No valuables were taken; the money in the house remained untouched. Yet no witness identified such a person entering or leaving, and no particular outsider was linked to the killings.

Lizzie remained the focus of suspicion for many people after the trial. The authorities never charged anyone else. The repeated search for another explanation did not produce a later prosecution.

For Lizzie and Emma, their father's death also brought a material change. They inherited from an estate valued at about $300,000 and left Second Street. In the Hill district, where Andrew had declined to live, they bought a modern fourteen-room Victorian house and named it Maplecroft.

The sisters now had the space and conveniences their father had chosen not to provide. They no longer lived in the partitioned house near the mills, and decisions about their home no longer depended on his approval.

But Lizzie did not regain her former place in Fall River society. Old acquaintances crossed the street to avoid her, and shopkeepers served her in cold silence. The exclusion extended to the town's churches. Wealth gave her a different house, not an escape from the name attached to the killings. Much of her later life was spent privately at Maplecroft, with carriage rides and occasional trips away.

Emma remained with her until 1905. Then, after a bitter dispute neither sister publicly explained, she packed her belongings, moved out, and left Fall River permanently. They never saw or spoke to each other again.

The reason for that separation became another invitation to speculation. The sisters themselves left no public explanation for the rupture in a relationship that had lasted through childhood, the murders, and the trial.

Lizzie died of pneumonia at Maplecroft on June 1, 1927. She was 66. Emma died nine days later. Both were buried side by side in the family plot at Oak Grove Cemetery in Fall River, only yards from Andrew and Abby.

The house on Second Street survived them. It is now a museum and historic inn. Visitors can enter the sitting room where Andrew lay and follow the separate routes upstairs. The front door opens into a narrow entry hall, where the steep front staircase leads directly to the second-floor landing beside the guest bedroom.

Among the people associated with those rooms, Abby is often remembered least. Her place in the public story became a relationship to be clarified, a count of wounds, or a line in a rhyme. Attention settled on the surviving daughter and the trial, while the first victim was reduced to part of the accusation.

Abby was 64 and had been married to Andrew for nearly three decades. Whatever the conflicting accounts reveal about the household's divisions, much of her life there had involved keeping it running. On the morning she died, a guest had stayed overnight, and his room needed attention.

At about nine on August 4, after the breakfast dishes had been cleared, she went up the front staircase alone to make the bed John Morse had used. Morse had gone to visit relatives. Andrew was leaving for his business downtown. Bridget had windows to wash.

Abby carried clean linens into the guest bedroom. The room overlooked the quiet street below on that hot morning. There was a coverlet to smooth, pillow shams to arrange, and furniture to dust. It was an ordinary chore in a house that had begun its day.

She never came back down.
