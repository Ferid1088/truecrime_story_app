[[ACT:act1]]

Andrew Borden was standing outside his own front door. Inside, the family’s maid, Bridget Sullivan, was struggling to let him in. The door was secured, the lock would not give, and Andrew waited while she worked at it.

Then, Bridget later testified, she heard a woman laugh at the top of the stairs. She said it was Andrew’s daughter, Lizzie.

Lizzie denied it. She had not laughed, she said, and she had not been upstairs. She was on the ground floor when her father came home.

The disagreement placed her in two different parts of a house where two people would be found dead: Andrew on the sitting room sofa, and his wife, Abby, in the guest room near the top of those stairs. Bridget’s recollection put Lizzie close to that room. Lizzie’s own account put her downstairs, away from it.

Later that morning, Bridget heard Lizzie calling her down. Someone had killed her father, Lizzie said. Bridget went to the sitting room. Andrew was lying on the sofa, his face and head so badly injured by repeated hatchet blows that officers would describe him as nearly unrecognizable.

Lizzie sent Bridget to fetch the family doctor, Dr. Bowen. He was not home, so Bridget left word with his wife. Meanwhile, the Bordens’ neighbor Adelaide Churchill saw Lizzie at the back door. Lizzie asked her to come over. Someone had killed her father.

Mrs. Churchill lived just north of the Borden property. She crossed to the house. When Bridget returned from looking for the doctor, she found Lizzie waiting near the open doorway.

There was still another member of the family to find. Abby had not been seen for some time. Earlier that morning, she had gone upstairs to put the guest room in order after an overnight visitor. Now her husband was dead, and she was nowhere downstairs.

Mrs. Churchill and Bridget went up the front staircase together. In the guest room, beside the bed, they found Abby lying face down on the floor. She, too, had been struck repeatedly in the head.

Andrew lay downstairs, his wife upstairs, and no witness had seen either attack. Neighbors and passers-by reportedly heard nothing. Nobody had seen a killer enter the house or leave it.

It was Thursday, August 4, 1892, in Fall River, Massachusetts, a mill town near the Rhode Island border. The Borden home stood at 92 Second Street, a short walk from the center of town.

Andrew was 70, a wealthy man whose frugality was well known. Abby, 64, was his second wife. His two unmarried daughters, Lizzie and Emma, lived with them, and Bridget, a 26-year-old Irish immigrant, worked and slept in the house.

That morning, Emma was away visiting in Fairhaven, about 15 miles from Fall River. The overnight guest, John Morse, had left after breakfast. Andrew had gone downtown on business before returning to the locked front door.

Apart from the victims, the only people known to have been in the house during the killings were Lizzie and Bridget. According to Bridget, she had been outside washing windows during one attack and upstairs resting in her room during the other.

Police found no sign of forced entry. Nothing had been reported stolen. There was no obvious robbery to account for the violence, and no witness who could describe the person responsible. To reconstruct the morning, investigators would have to rely heavily on the people who had survived it.

Police arrived late that morning, with many officers away at the department’s annual picnic. Those who came found Andrew on the sofa and were taken upstairs to Abby. Soon the house was full of people coming and going: officers, neighbors, friends, and others drawn by the news.

The violence was unmistakable. The circumstances were much harder to understand. Two people had been killed in daylight inside a house whose occupants kept its doors locked. A daughter and a maid had been there, yet neither said she had witnessed an attack.

No one was ever convicted of the murders of Andrew and Abby Borden, and the killings have never been officially solved.

The disagreement at the front door would remain part of the case. Bridget placed Lizzie near the guest room. Lizzie placed herself downstairs. Both accounts came from women who knew the house, its stairs, and the people who lived behind that door.

[[ACT:act2]]

Lizzie Andrew Borden was born in Fall River on July 19, 1860. Her father, Andrew Jackson Borden, and his first wife, Sarah, already had an older daughter, Emma, about nine years her senior. Sarah died when Lizzie was a small child. In June 1865, Andrew married Abby Durfee Gray.

By the summer of 1892, Abby had belonged to the household for 27 years. Lizzie was 32, Emma 41. Both daughters were unmarried and still living with their father.

Andrew had begun by making furniture and coffins. Over time, he accumulated interests in real estate, banks, and textile mills, becoming one of Fall River’s wealthiest men. His home gave little indication of that success. Families with comparable means tended to live in the fashionable neighborhood on the hill. Andrew remained on Second Street, in a modest house without indoor plumbing.

His care with money had become part of his reputation. Many people in town considered him a miser, and he was not widely popular. The family’s circumstances reflected his choices: substantial wealth, a relatively plain home, and none of the move to the Hill that his fortune would have allowed.

The Bordens attended Central Congregational Church. Lizzie taught Sunday school and helped with charitable work, including a flower committee and work connected with the local hospital. Before the killings, her public identity was familiar and respectable: a churchgoing woman who lived at home and took part in the community’s good works. She had also traveled to Europe in 1890.

Abby is harder to distinguish from the accounts later given about her. Much of what survives concerns her place in the family, particularly the daughters’ feelings toward her. Some accounts say Lizzie believed Abby had married Andrew for his money. Others describe a less hostile relationship. Lizzie herself would call it cordial.

Both daughters called her Mrs. Borden rather than mother. On the day of the murders, when an officer referred to Abby as Lizzie’s mother, Lizzie corrected him: “She is not my mother, sir. She is my father’s wife. My mother died when I was a child.”

The words establish a distinction Lizzie wanted understood. They do not, by themselves, tell us how the two women lived together. Lizzie had lost Sarah when she was very young, and Abby, despite her long place in the household, was not someone she chose to call mother.

Bridget would say that she never witnessed family quarrels. She also described a household in which the daughters rarely ate with their parents and the women took care of their own rooms. Other people remembered bitterness that was less apparent to the maid. A dressmaker and a woman who had traveled with Lizzie would later attribute openly hostile remarks about Abby to her.

Money and property lay behind some of those accounts. Andrew gave real estate to members of Abby’s family, and his daughters wanted property of their own. A frequently repeated story dates a feud to 1887, when Andrew bought a house for Abby’s half-sister to occupy.

Lizzie was reported to have complained, “What he did for her people he ought to do for his own children.” That remark and the story of the feud come through weaker, secondhand reporting. They have often been used to explain the household’s divisions, but they cannot carry the same weight as the property transfers themselves.

Andrew did sell each daughter a house for $1. The sisters later sold the houses back to him for $5,000 apiece. The transactions show that the daughters pressed for property and received it. Whether the arrangement settled their resentment is less clear. Accounts of their feelings toward Andrew and Abby still differed sharply after the deaths.

The house imposed divisions of its own. It had been built for two families, with rooms opening into one another and several interior doors kept locked. Upstairs, there was no hallway connecting all the bedrooms. Moving between parts of the house could require going downstairs and coming up again by a different route.

To get from Lizzie’s bedroom to Andrew’s, a person had to go down the front staircase, cross the house, and climb the back stairs off the kitchen. The front stairs served the guest room and the daughters’ rooms. The back stairs led to Andrew and Abby’s bedroom and to Bridget’s quarters. From the front landing, someone could see into the guest room.

The Bordens were known for keeping their doors secured. Their home was small enough for family members to live close to one another, but its layout kept their rooms apart. The route a person took through it could determine whom they passed and which rooms they saw.

Bridget had worked for the Bordens for about three years. She had emigrated from Ireland in 1883 and lived in the attic. Lizzie called her Maggie, a name one writer attributes to a previous maid. Her work carried her through the household, though her testimony suggests that its members often kept to themselves.

On August 3, another person joined them. John Morse was the brother of Andrew’s first wife, Sarah, and therefore the daughters’ maternal uncle. He was a farmer who had spent much of his life in Iowa. Some accounts describe his arrival as unexpected. He was invited to stay for a few days and discuss business with Andrew, and he slept in the guest room.

The family was also dealing with illness. Andrew, Abby, and Bridget had suffered stomach trouble, blamed on mutton left out overnight in the summer heat. The house had an icebox, but no refrigerator. By the day before the murders, Andrew had come to suspect that someone might have tried to poison the household. No evidence confirmed that fear, and later examination of the victims’ stomachs found no poison.

That evening, Lizzie visited her friend Alice Russell. According to Russell’s later account, Lizzie was low in spirits and spoke of an unknown threat hanging over her. Something was going to happen, she said, though she did not know what or when. She mentioned that her father had enemies.

Those words would acquire a different significance after the deaths. Russell could describe what Lizzie had said; she could not explain what lay behind it.

There was another report from August 3. A drugstore clerk, Eli Bence, said a woman had asked him to sell her prussic acid, a deadly poison. He identified her as Lizzie Borden. According to Bence, she said she needed it to clean a sealskin cape, and he refused to provide it without a prescription.

Accounts differ over the details of the request, sometimes describing furs or a coat. Lizzie denied making the attempt. Her attorneys said Bence had mistaken another woman for her, and his identification was never conclusively established. Whatever had happened in the drugstore, poison was not the means by which Andrew and Abby died.

Yet the report placed another disputed event on the day before the killings. Bence said he had refused a lethal drug to Lizzie Borden. Lizzie said she had never asked for it.

[[ACT:act3]]

Breakfast on August 4 is described differently by different sources. Some place Andrew, Abby, and Lizzie together at about seven. Others include John Morse and Bridget, or say Lizzie came down later and ate alone. The accounts become clearer as people began leaving the house.

Morse departed at about 8:45 to visit relatives across town. He intended to return around noon. Andrew went downtown on business at about nine. Emma was already away in Fairhaven.

Abby told Bridget to wash the windows, inside and out. The maid was still feeling the effects of the household’s illness, but she began the work. Abby went up the front stairs to make the bed in the guest room where Morse had slept. That is the last activity for which any account confirms her alive.

Lizzie later said she spent part of the morning ironing handkerchiefs. She also told Bridget about a sale at a local department store and said she could go shopping. Bridget did not go.

Andrew returned at about 10:30 and found the front door locked. Bridget admitted him. After he came inside, he eventually lay down on the sitting room sofa. Bridget, her window washing finished and still feeling unwell, went up to her attic room to rest. Lizzie’s call brought her down again.

Accounts put the number of hatchet blows to Andrew’s face and head at ten or 11. Abby’s injuries are generally counted as about 18 or 19. Both had been attacked repeatedly with a heavy, sharp weapon, in separate rooms.

Parts of Andrew’s face had been destroyed. One eye was split, and blood was found on the sofa and floor. Some accounts infer that he was asleep when the attack began, partly from the injury to his eye. No witness could confirm that. His position and wounds helped investigators reconstruct the attack.

Abby lay face down beside the guest room bed. Her wounds were concentrated at the back of her head. Reconstructions suggest that an initial blow may have struck near the side of her head, causing her to fall, and that further blows were delivered after she was down. Bruising to her nose and forehead was also described.

Nobody had seen the first blow or could say how the attacker approached her. The injuries allowed examiners to propose a sequence, but not to identify the person who had stood beside the bed.

What the bodies did establish was an order: Abby had died first.

Her blood was dark, dry, and clotted, and her body was cold. Andrew’s blood was still wet, and his body remained warm to the touch. The contrast gave examiners a clear reason to conclude that the deaths had occurred some time apart.

The interval is usually estimated at about an hour and a half. Other estimates range from an hour to nearly two hours. One reported coroner’s estimate placed Abby’s death between 9:00 and 10:30 and Andrew’s between 10:30 and 11:10. Those were broad medical estimates. The exact windows come through individual accounts and should not be mistaken for times recorded by a witness.

Andrew’s movements provide an independent limit: he could not have been killed before returning home at about 10:30. Abby’s last confirmed activity was making the guest room bed earlier in the morning. By the time Andrew came back from his errands, she was probably already dead upstairs.

Investigators considered whether the killer had remained inside for as long as 90 minutes, waiting for Andrew. That was an inference from the separation between the deaths. It did not establish where the attacker went during the interval, or whether that person had left and returned.

For police, the timing made a single morning into two distinct periods to explain. During the earlier one, Andrew was downtown and Bridget was occupied with the windows. During the later one, Andrew was home and Bridget said she was resting. Abby’s body remained in the guest room through both.

Officers searched the house for a weapon. In the cellar, they found two axes, two hatchets, and a hatchet head with a broken handle, lying in a box of tools. Several officers thought the exposed break in the wood looked fresh. The blade appeared to have been cleaned, and ash covered it.

The prosecution would identify this handleless hatchet as the likely weapon. Its blade was about three and a half inches wide, a size apparently consistent with the wounds. Investigators proposed that the killer had broken off the handle and rubbed the blade with ash to disguise what it had done.

That explanation depended on the object’s condition being unusual. Defense testimony described a substantial accumulation of ash in the cellar that coated everything. The ash on the hatchet could have come from lying among the other tools. The testimony about the handle was also contested. A fresh-looking break gave investigators a reason for suspicion, but deliberate removal to conceal a murder remained their theory.

A Harvard chemist examined two axes and two hatchets from the cellar and reported finding no blood. No identifiable blood linked the hatchet head to either victim. Its dimensions made it a possible weapon, but the available methods could not match a particular blade to these wounds with certainty. The hatchet head, now held by the Fall River Historical Society, has never been established as the murder weapon.

Investigators also found a bucket of bloodstained rags, placed in the basement by some accounts. Lizzie said the blood came from her menstrual period. According to one account, her doctor supported that explanation. The rags never became a demonstrated connection between her and the attacks.

The search had limits that could not be repaired afterward. In the first hours, nobody checked Lizzie’s underclothes for stains or examined her for wounds. Friends, neighbors, and others moved through the house while police were trying to understand it. The scene had not been preserved in a way that kept every object and every trace undisturbed.

There were also limits to the science. Testing of the recovered tools did not establish that the broken-handled hatchet was the murder weapon. The different condition of the victims’ blood helped establish that they had died at different times. It could not reveal who had struck them.

Several accounts describe Lizzie’s hands, hair, and visible clothing as free of blood when police saw her. No bloodstained dress, apron, or glove was found that morning and tied to the attacks. That absence mattered, though it came from an investigation that had already left some things unchecked.

More than an hour was believed to separate the two killings. Morse had left before either was thought to have occurred. Bridget placed herself at work outside and later resting upstairs. Lizzie’s whereabouts during the critical periods depended largely on what she said.

Someone had either entered and moved through the house without being seen, or had already been inside it. No object or stain recovered there could tie either death to a person. Police had to turn from what they could find to what the survivors could tell them.

[[ACT:act4]]

Lizzie gave police an account on the day of the murders. Her stepmother, she said, had gone out after receiving a note asking her to visit a sick friend. Bridget was washing windows. When Andrew came home and asked where Abby was, Lizzie told him about the message.

Andrew then lay down on the sitting room sofa. Lizzie said she went outside to the barn for about 20 minutes, looking for a piece of iron. When she returned, she found her father dead and called for Bridget.

Some officers were troubled by her manner. Their reports described her as too calm or too poised. Other observers also remembered unusual composure. Those impressions helped shape the suspicion directed toward her, although they could not establish what she had done. The officers had no reliable measure of how Lizzie would behave after finding her father killed.

The story about Abby offered something more concrete to investigate. A message had supposedly arrived, carrying a request important enough for her to leave the house. Police searched for it. None was produced, and no sick friend came forward to explain the request. According to one account, Lizzie later said Abby had burned the note.

What Lizzie said after finding Andrew introduced a different possibility. According to people at the house, she suggested that Abby was upstairs. She was reported to have said she thought she had heard Abby come in and that someone should look for her there.

Bridget remembered being told earlier that Abby had been called away to visit a sick neighbor. Now Lizzie was speaking as though she might be back. No one independently established that Abby had returned from such a visit. Another account says Lizzie stopped Bridget when the maid first tried to find Abby to tell her Andrew was dead.

A return could have reconciled Abby being away with her being upstairs. It remained Lizzie’s suggestion, however, rather than a movement anyone else had confirmed. Police could find neither the person who had supposedly summoned Abby nor the message that had taken her out.

The discrepancy concerned more than an imprecise recollection of time. While Andrew was alive and asking for his wife, Lizzie had supplied a reason for her absence. After he was dead, she directed attention toward the part of the house where Abby would be found. Whether those statements came from confusion in the aftermath or from knowledge she had not disclosed was something the statements alone could not settle.

Another detail concerned what happened when Andrew prepared to rest.

In the account attributed to Lizzie, she helped her father settle on the sofa. Some versions say she helped him take off his boots. An account of Bridget’s testimony supplies a different version, in which Bridget helped him remove his boots and put on slippers.

These descriptions differ over who performed the action as well as what Andrew wore afterward. They concern an ordinary household moment, the sort of routine that could help someone remember when she last saw him alive.

The body supplied a check. Andrew was found with his boots still on. Crime scene photographs show them, and several written accounts describe them. The slippers version did not match what police found on the sofa.

A witness could recall a familiar routine incorrectly or confuse one day with another. But removing his footwear was a particular action Lizzie was reported to have described, and it had not happened as that description suggested. Unlike an impression of her manner, the boots gave investigators something they could compare directly with her words.

The people who had seen Lizzie were also asked what she had been wearing. Bridget remembered a blue dress. At first, that recollection was simply another detail in the account of the day.

Then, three days after the killings, a dress was destroyed.

On Sunday, August 7, Alice Russell came into the Borden kitchen. Emma had returned from Fairhaven and was there with Lizzie. Russell later testified that Lizzie was standing at the stove, tearing a dress into strips.

Russell knew the garment. She described a cheap cotton dress, light blue with a small dark figure, a Bedford cord she had seen Lizzie wear in early spring. Her memory was of a particular dress she had encountered before, rather than merely a blue garment seen at a distance.

When Emma asked what she was doing, Lizzie answered, according to Russell, “I am going to burn this old thing up. It is covered with paint.”

Russell saw no blood. She remembered soil along the edge. She also remembered warning Lizzie: “If I were you I wouldn’t let anybody see me do that, Lizzie.”

Russell did not see the garment actually put into the stove. Her testimony described the dress, the tearing, and Lizzie’s stated intention. Emma would acknowledge its disposal and say that getting rid of the dress had been her own suggestion. In her account, she had advised Lizzie to destroy an old, stained garment.

Police were stationed outside the house. The sisters were still living where the killings had occurred, with a friend present and investigators continuing their work. Russell’s warning showed that she understood how destroying a dress under those circumstances could look.

For a time, she kept what she had seen to herself. The exchange did not immediately become part of what investigators knew. It remained with a friend who had heard Lizzie speak of danger before the deaths and was now staying in the house afterward.

Her recollection would eventually give police something different from a disputed answer or an impression of Lizzie’s composure. It described an action: Lizzie tearing up a dress at the kitchen stove within days of the murders. Emma’s account placed that action in the disposal of spoiled clothing. Russell’s warning captured how troubling it appeared even then.

Bridget remembered blue clothing on the morning of August 4. Russell described a light-blue dress she recognized from earlier in the year. Nobody could establish that the destroyed garment was the one Lizzie had worn that morning.

Lizzie said it was covered with paint. Emma said she had advised getting rid of it. The dress itself was gone, beyond anyone’s ability to examine.

[[ACT:act5]]

For a week after the murders, Lizzie remained free. She stayed in the Second Street house with Emma and a friend while officers continued their investigation and watched from outside.

The funeral came and went. Police searched the house again on August 5. One account says Lizzie was identified as the main suspect that evening. By Saturday evening, the mayor and the lead investigator were reported to have told her and others in the house that they were officially under suspicion.

No charge had yet been filed. Lizzie could move about her home and speak with the people staying there, but the officials around her were increasingly trying to decide whether she should be arrested. The search and questioning continued in the same rooms where the family had lived before Thursday.

Outside, Fall River was already arguing about the case. Rumor spread through the town and into the newspapers. Some people believed Lizzie had killed her father and stepmother; others believed suspicion was being mistaken for evidence. She was still at home, but her private life and the deaths inside it were becoming public property.

Then officials required her to appear at a closed inquest. The inquest lasted three days. Lizzie appeared without legal counsel beside her and without being warned that she could remain silent. The procedure was legal at the time.

She was also taking medication. Dr. Bowen had prescribed morphine for her nerves after the killings. Her attorney would later say she was taking it regularly and was under its influence at the hearing. Bowen testified that he had prescribed a double dose after the murders and that the drug’s side effects could account for confusion.

In that room, the officials wanted a detailed reconstruction. They asked where she had been when Andrew returned, what she had done before finding him, and why she had gone outside. Her answers would be heard by the same authorities deciding whether the investigation should become a prosecution.

Under questioning, her account shifted.

When describing Andrew’s return, she first placed herself on the stairs. Later in the same testimony, she placed herself in the kitchen. In accounts of the period before she discovered him, she also described eating pears in the yard. Other tellings put her in the barn loft eating them.

The distinction in time matters. Moving from one place to another over the course of a morning was ordinary. The difficulty for her questioners was that her answers did not give them a consistent account of where she had been at the moments they were asking about.

Her reason for going to the barn changed as well. In one version, she had gone looking for a piece of iron or tin to repair a screen. In another, she was looking for fishing sinkers and intended to go fishing in Fairhaven.

That errand explained her absence when her father was killed. Her account had allowed about 20 minutes outside. The officials were trying to determine what she had done during that interval and whether anyone or anything supported her description.

An officer who inspected the loft reported dust on the floor and no footprints. In his assessment, it showed no sign of anyone having been there for weeks. The loft was also described as stiflingly hot. Police used those observations to challenge the trip, though the claim of undisturbed dust remained the officer’s report.

When confronted with differences in what she had said, Lizzie answered, “I don’t know what I have said.”

At another point she told her questioners, “Some of your questions I have difficulty answering because I don’t know just how you mean them.”

Sources describe her as erratic at times and sometimes confrontational. She revised answers, disputed the way questions were put, and struggled to explain earlier statements. The officials heard contradictions. Her defenders would see a grieving woman being questioned without a lawyer while taking morphine. Her answers do not reveal how much came from confusion, medication, strain, or evasion.

What had changed was the status of those answers. Police had first spoken with her amid the arrivals and discoveries at the house. Now she had given a sworn account in a formal proceeding, before officials who were weighing a charge against her.

By one account, the judge, district attorney, and police marshal concluded that she was “probably guilty” because they could not consistently account for her movements that morning.

On August 11, 1892, one week after the killings, Lizzie Borden was arrested and charged with murdering her father and stepmother.

She had been charged, not convicted. The decision meant officials believed there was enough to bring a case against her. Their judgment would now have to be tested through the courts.

According to one account, she pleaded not guilty the following day. She was taken to jail in Taunton, where she would remain through the autumn and winter while the case moved toward trial. The woman who had spent the first week at home under police observation was now in custody.

The prosecution still had to take the charges before a grand jury, the panel that decides whether a case should proceed. In December, Alice Russell told that panel about the dress. She testified on December 1. On December 2, the grand jury indicted Lizzie on two counts of murder.

Russell’s disclosure brought something she had witnessed into the formal case, months after she had stood in the Borden kitchen. The friend who had heard Lizzie speak of an unknown threat before the murders was now describing what she had seen after them.

A trial was set for the following June, almost a year after the killings. Newspapers far beyond Fall River were following the case. Accounts of the household, the investigation, and Lizzie’s behavior had circulated for months, reaching readers who knew her chiefly as the woman accused of killing her parents.

The officials had heard her struggle to explain the morning. A grand jury had now allowed the prosecution to go forward. Whether the trial jury would be permitted to hear her inquest answers was still to be decided.

[[ACT:act6]]

Lizzie Borden’s trial opened on June 5, 1893, in the Superior Court at New Bedford, Massachusetts. Nearly ten months had passed since the killings.

Three judges sat on the bench: Caleb Blodget, Justin Dewey, and Albert Mason. District Attorney Hosea Knowlton led the prosecution, with William Moody beside him. Andrew Jennings represented Lizzie for the defense, joined by George Robinson, a former governor of Massachusetts.

The jury consisted of twelve men, selected from about 150 prospective jurors. They were mostly farmers and tradesmen, and none came from Fall River. Women could not serve on Massachusetts juries at the time. A murder conviction carried a mandatory death sentence.

Reporters arrived from Boston and New York. Newspapers across the country followed the proceedings, and crowds gathered outside the courthouse. The private life of the Second Street household, already examined for months, would now be described by witnesses before the men who would decide Lizzie’s fate.

Inside the courtroom, the victims’ skulls were introduced as exhibits. They had been removed during a second autopsy. Several sources report that Lizzie fainted during the trial. Other accounts describe her composure as something that helped her with the jury. The same restraint some officers had considered suspicious in August could be interpreted differently in court.

The prosecution’s theory joined motive to opportunity. It would argue that Lizzie resented her stepmother, had a financial interest in her father’s death, and had been in the house during the critical hours. The defense challenged the state to turn those circumstances into proof that she had committed both murders.

But the case the jury heard was narrower than the one investigators had assembled.

The judges excluded Lizzie’s inquest testimony because she had given it without a lawyer and without being warned of her right to remain silent. The prosecution therefore could not present her shifting sworn account as evidence. The questioning that had helped officials decide to arrest her would not be repeated for the jury.

Lizzie did not take the stand at trial. The jurors would not hear her answer another series of questions about the morning or watch her respond to the prosecution’s challenges. Before they retired, one account records her saying, “I am innocent. I leave it to my counsel to speak for me.”

The pharmacist’s account of the attempted prussic acid purchase was also excluded. The reported reasons for the ruling include the absence of a connection between poison and these deaths, and the state’s failure to rule out an innocent use. The jury did not hear Eli Bence’s identification.

That removed a possible suggestion of preparation from the prosecution’s case. Investigators had considered the drugstore report alongside the household’s illness and the murders that followed. The court required a basis for admitting it as evidence in this prosecution, and the clerk’s story did not pass that test. His identification and the defense’s claim of mistaken identity stayed outside the jury’s consideration.

The dress remained part of the case, but prosecutors did not substantiate their assertion that it was the garment Lizzie wore on August 4. They could ask jurors to infer concealment from its destruction; they could not supply that connection.

These were different kinds of limits. The inquest and drugstore accounts were excluded by the court. The difficulty with the dress was a failure of proof within evidence the jurors did hear. Together, they changed how much of the investigators’ accusation could be put before the twelve men.

Bridget’s account of Andrew’s return reached them.

She described struggling with the front door and hearing Lizzie laugh from the top of the stairs. She also supplied the small detail that explained why she remembered the moment: she had cursed at the stubborn lock, and the laugh seemed to follow her curse.

That was Bridget’s own explanation for the laughter. It gave the sound an immediate, ordinary cause. Its importance to the case lay in where she said it came from: upstairs, close to the guest room where Abby had been killed.

Lizzie had denied laughing and denied being upstairs. Bridget placed her there when Andrew came home. The jurors had to assess the maid’s recollection without hearing Lizzie questioned about it in court.

Other testimony concerned the family’s relationships. Witnesses said Lizzie hated Abby. A dressmaker repeated a hostile remark she attributed to Lizzie after referring to Abby as her mother. Someone who had traveled with Lizzie in Europe had reported a similar sentiment. Bridget described the daughters’ habit of eating separately from their parents.

The defense also drew on Bridget. She said she had never witnessed the family quarreling. Her answer gave the jury a different view from the openly embittered household suggested by some prosecution witnesses. People who had known the family could describe distance and resentment without agreeing on how those feelings were expressed.

That disagreement mattered to the prosecution’s account of motive. The state wanted jurors to see a depth of hostility that could explain the attacks. The defense could point to a woman who had worked inside the home for years and said she had not seen the family fight.

Alice Russell testified for the prosecution on June 8. Her account of the dress gave Knowlton an act he could present as the destruction of possible evidence. Her warning to Lizzie showed that someone present had immediately recognized how suspicious it could appear.

Emma testified for the defense. She took responsibility for suggesting that the old dress be discarded and described it as faded and stained. A painter and a dressmaker supported the claim that Lizzie had gotten paint on a dress.

The defense also asked the jurors to consider the openness of the disposal. Would a woman knowingly destroying bloody evidence do it in her own kitchen, with a friend present and police outside? The prosecution treated the act as incriminating. The defense put it in the context of spoiled clothing that Emma herself had advised her sister to discard.

The two sides were drawing opposite meanings from the same event. Russell’s presence could make the action seem especially reckless, or especially unlikely to be concealment. Emma’s explanation supplied a household reason for it. The jury had to decide what weight the act deserved.

Social expectations entered the arguments as well. The defense asked whether a respectable, churchgoing woman could have inflicted such violence. The prosecution, according to one account, appealed to stereotypes about women’s capacity for cunning. Both sides asked the all-male jury to consider what sort of woman Lizzie was, drawing different conclusions from her sex and social position.

Historian Joseph Conforti has identified gender, social class, and nativism among the forces shaping the acquittal, alongside reasonable doubt. Those are later interpretations of the trial. They describe assumptions available to lawyers and jurors, but they cannot tell us precisely what moved each man who voted.

The prosecution took nine days to present its case; the defense took two. The state had offered witnesses who described the house, the family’s relationships, and Lizzie’s behavior. The defense’s shorter presentation challenged the conclusions the state wanted drawn from them.

On June 20, Judge Justin Dewey instructed the men before they retired. He described a case based on circumstantial evidence and treated inconsistent statements as understandable under the circumstances. Suspicion, he told them, even a strong probability of guilt, was insufficient. Unless the state had met its burden of proof, they had to acquit.

Their deliberation was short, generally reported as about 90 minutes, though some accounts give less. They returned with a unanimous decision.

The foreman announced the verdict: not guilty.

Lizzie was acquitted of both murders. People in the courtroom stood and cheered. The New York Times reported that the cheering carried through the open windows and could be heard about half a mile away. Beyond the courthouse, opinion remained sharply divided, in Fall River and across the country.

The state had presented an accusation built on motive, opportunity, and disputed circumstances without supplying a conclusive link between Lizzie and either attack. It had no eyewitness to the killings and no confession. Important parts of what investigators had considered damaging had never reached the jury.

The verdict was a finding that the charges had not been proved. Not guilty was not proof of innocence, and it was not proof that anyone else was guilty. Having been acquitted, Lizzie could not be tried again for the same killings.

She walked out of the courthouse free.

Long before the verdict, the story had begun to escape the facts. Newspapers could present Lizzie as a coldblooded killer or as an innocent woman pursued by an investigation that would not let her go. Some went further and invented material outright.

Boston Globe reporter Henry Trickey published a fabricated front-page account of the case. On October 10, the paper carried a claim that Lizzie had been pregnant and that Andrew had threatened to evict her. Two days later, the Globe retracted the story as a hoax and apologized for slandering her virtue.

Readers had been offered a private scandal as an explanation for two real deaths. The accusation gave the violence a motive that depended on events that had not happened. The correction followed, but the invented story had already circulated.

The rhyme made an even more durable version: Lizzie Borden took an axe, gave her mother forty whacks, then gave her father forty-one. Its rhythm preserved errors about the weapon, the family relationship, and the number of blows. Abby was Lizzie’s stepmother, the weapon was a hatchet, and the attacks did not involve anything like the rhyme’s total.

It also turned an accusation into a certainty. A complicated case became a few lines that named a killer, supplied a weapon, and reduced two people’s deaths to something easy to repeat.

Film and television dramatizations have continued turning the case into stories with motives, relationships, and answers that the historical record does not establish.

A different kind of supposed answer came in reports about Bridget Sullivan. One version says she confessed on her deathbed that she had lied to help secure Lizzie’s acquittal. Another says that, dying in Butte in 1948, she told her sister she had changed her testimony in Lizzie’s favor.

These reports are secondhand and have never been conclusively verified. They do not identify a reliably documented statement taken from Bridget at the time. Another account says she maintained her story throughout her life. Her sworn testimony remains on the record; the alleged retraction remains a rumor.

The disagreement about the staircase has never been resolved by a confirmed later account from Bridget.

Other theories have named people close to the household, or imagined someone the witnesses never saw.

John Morse drew early attention because of his visit and his stay in the guest room. Police questioned him, but witnesses corroborated his movements across town. No evidence established that he planned or participated in the killings. His presence the night before did not place him in the house during the attacks.

An outside intruder remained another possibility. Police arrested an innocent Portuguese immigrant within hours of the discoveries. A bloody axe found on a neighboring farm had been used on chickens. Neither lead produced a killer.

A stranger would have had to move through a house whose occupants kept its doors locked, and account for the interval between the attacks. No intruder was identified as entering or leaving. Nothing had been reported stolen. The theory supplied a person outside the household without establishing who that person was or how the movements had occurred.

Other accusations have involved Emma, Bridget, or unproven abuse by Andrew. One theory named William, supposedly Andrew’s illegitimate son; later research showed that he was not Andrew’s son, and nothing placed him in the house. None of these explanations has been established by historical evidence.

Lizzie remained, for many people, the principal suspect after her acquittal. That enduring suspicion did not produce a new prosecution. No one else was ever charged.

The sisters inherited a substantial part of Andrew’s estate, estimated at about $300,000. In 1893, they bought a large house on the Hill, the fashionable neighborhood their father had declined to live in. Lizzie named it Maplecroft.

It offered comforts absent from Second Street, including indoor plumbing, along with a staff of maids and a coachman. After years in their father’s modest home, the sisters could live in the neighborhood and circumstances his fortune had always made possible.

Lizzie stayed in Fall River. Many neighbors and members of her church kept their distance. Accounts describe shopkeepers serving her without speaking to her. The woman who had taught Sunday school and worked on church charities no longer occupied her former place in the town.

Money changed where she lived and how comfortably she could live there. It did not restore those relationships. Maplecroft became her home, while the events on Second Street continued to define how people spoke about her.

In 1905, after a quarrel over a party Lizzie gave for the actress Nance O’Neil, Emma moved out. The sisters never reconciled. Emma had testified for Lizzie at trial, and in 1913 she again said she did not believe her sister had committed the murders.

Lizzie died at Maplecroft on June 1, 1927, at 66. Emma died nine days later. Both were buried in the family plot beside Andrew and Abby.

The house on Second Street is now a museum and bed and breakfast. Visitors can enter rooms furnished in the style of the 1890s and sit on a sofa like the one where Andrew was found. They can also go upstairs to the guest room. The house has become a place associated above all with the daughter who survived it.

Abby is remembered more faintly. She was 64, and she had spent 27 years in the Borden household. Much of her surviving identity comes through other people’s accounts of whether the daughters liked her, what Andrew gave her relatives, and what the family called her.

On the morning of August 4, she had a bed to make. John Morse had slept in the guest room, and she went upstairs to put it in order. Bridget was washing windows. Andrew was downtown. Abby was carrying out an ordinary task in a house where she had lived for years.

She was the first to die. Her body remained beside the bed while Andrew finished his errands and returned to the front door.
